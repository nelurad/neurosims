The Spotlight of Attention

Your eyes take in a whole messy world, but you only really notice a little piece of it at a time. This sim is about how the brain picks that piece: on purpose, or because something grabbed it.

    Start here

    A flashlight in a dark room

    In plain wordsAttention works a bit like a flashlight. Whatever is in the beam gets seen clearly. Everything else is still there, but it's dim and fuzzy.

    A ladybug is hiding somewhere on this desk. Move your mouse (or drag a finger) to shine the light around, and click the ladybug when you find it.

    Find the ladybug 🐞
    Try a huge beam. You light up more of the desk, but everything gets blurrier. That's the zoom-lens idea: spread attention wide and you lose detail, focus it tight and you see fine detail in a small area (Eriksen & St. James, 1986).

    Part 1

    Who aims the flashlight?

    In plain wordsSometimes you point the flashlight ("where's my friend?"). Sometimes the world grabs it ("what was that noise?!"). The brain uses two different teams for these.
    Dorsal network (on top)Ventral network (lower down)Visual cortex

    Right hemisphere, seen from the right side. Front of the brain is on the right.

    Pick a story and watch which team lights up.

    Dorsal = "I choose"

    Top-down, goal-driven

    Two hubs: the intraparietal sulcus and superior parietal lobule up in the parietal lobe, and the frontal eye fields in the frontal lobe. They hold your goal and keep the beam where you want it. Both hemispheres have one.

    Ventral = "Hey, look!"

    Bottom-up, stimulus-driven

    Hubs: the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and the ventral frontal cortex. Mostly on the right side. It stays quiet until something unexpected and important shows up, then it acts like a circuit breaker that interrupts the dorsal network and swings attention over.

    This two-network picture comes from Corbetta and Shulman (2002), based on many fMRI studies. In real life the two teams talk to each other constantly. The ventral network mostly responds to surprises that matter for your goal, and the exact jobs of the TPJ are still debated (it is also active when we think about other people's minds).

    Part 2

    The cue game

    In plain wordsIf someone points to where something will appear, you react faster. If they point the wrong way, you're slower, because your attention has to unstick, move, and land again.

    This is Michael Posner's classic task (1980). Keep your eyes on the + in the middle. When a ⭐ appears in either box, press Space or hit the yellow button as fast as you can.

    Cue type:

    Your reaction times

    Play a round. Blue = the cue pointed the right way. Pink = it pointed the wrong way.

    With the arrow, the cue is right 80% of the time, so it pays to follow it on purpose (endogenous, top-down). The flash is right only half the time, so it tells you nothing, yet it still speeds you up when the star comes quickly after it (exogenous, bottom-up). On wrong-cue trials, fMRI shows extra activity in the right TPJ: the ventral network catching the mistake and helping you reorient. Your numbers will bounce around with only 20 trials; that's normal.

    Part 3

    Some things pop out, some don't

    In plain wordsA red thing among blue things jumps out at you instantly. But a red T hiding among blue Ts and red Xs? Now you have to search, one by one.

    Click the red T as fast as you can. Play both versions and watch what happens to your time as the crowd grows.

    Time to find it

    Easy (pop-out)Tricky (conjunction)
    Play at least a couple of rounds of each.
    💡 The aha: In the easy version the line stays flat. Color is a basic feature the visual system checks everywhere at once, so the red T pops out no matter how many blue letters there are. In the tricky version, each extra letter adds time. To find "red and T," attention has to glue the features together, spot by spot. That's Anne Treisman's feature integration theory (Treisman & Gelade, 1980).

    Some of your time is just moving the mouse, so your lines sit higher than in a lab where people press a key. Later work showed search is rarely perfectly one-by-one: basic features can guide attention toward likely spots (Wolfe's "guided search"). But the big difference between pop-out and conjunction search holds up.

    Part 4

    Right in front of you

    In plain wordsWhen your attention is busy, you can miss something big and weird, even when you're looking right at it. It's called inattentional blindness.

    Your job: count how many times the pink balls bounce off the walls. Ignore the blue ones. It takes 15 seconds.

    How many pink bounces did you count?

    Did you notice anything unusual?

    In a famous study, about half of the people counting basketball passes missed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the game (Simons & Chabris, 1999). Missing things is more likely when the task is hard and when the surprise looks unlike what you're tracking. If you already know about this kind of demo, you probably caught it. That's attention too: you were expecting a trick.

    Part 5

    When half the world disappears

    In plain wordsAfter a stroke in the right parietal lobe, some people stop paying attention to the left side of everything. Their eyes work fine. The left side just doesn't seem to matter or exist. This is hemispatial neglect.

    Cross out every line

    Click each little line to cross it out (Albert's test, 1973). Then see how a patient with left neglect might do.

    Damage near the right TPJ and inferior parietal lobe, a hub of the ventral network, is a common cause.

    Why mostly the left side? One popular explanation: the right hemisphere keeps track of both sides of space, while the left hemisphere mainly covers the right side (Mesulam, 1981). Lose the left hemisphere's coverage and the right still has you covered. Lose the right, and nobody is watching the left.

    Patients may shave or put makeup on only one side of the face, eat from only one side of the plate, or draw a clock with all the numbers crammed onto the right. Many recover a lot over the first months. The parietal lobe isn't the only site; frontal and subcortical damage can cause neglect too.
