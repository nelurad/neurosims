From Light to Sight

Seeing feels instant, but it’s a relay with many stages. Light gets focused, turned into electrical signals, compared and sharpened in the retina, split and crossed on its way into the brain, broken into lines and edges, and finally rebuilt into objects you can name and reach for. 2Transduction: light turns a photoreceptor off

Photoreceptors at the back of the retina convert light into an electrical signal. The surprise: in the dark they are depolarized and constantly releasing glutamate (the “dark current”). Light hyperpolarizes them and reduces glutamate release. Turn up the light and follow the cascade.

  1. Photon absorbed by a photopigment (rhodopsin in rods). Its retinal flips from 11-cis to all-trans.
  2. Transducin, a G protein, is switched on.
  3. Transducin activates PDE, an enzyme that breaks down cGMP.
  4. Less cGMP, so cGMP-gated Na⁺ channels close. The dark current stops.
  5. The cell hyperpolarizes (about −40 → −70 mV).
  6. Less glutamate is released onto bipolar cells. That drop is the message “light!”

3The retina compares: center vs. surround

Signals flow from photoreceptors to bipolar cells to retinal ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve. Each ganglion cell watches a small patch of the world, its receptive field, split into a center and a surround that work against each other. Drag the light around and listen to the cell fire.

4Crossing over: eye to thalamus to cortex

Each eye sees both halves of the world. At the optic chiasm, fibers from the inner (nasal) half of each retina cross, so everything in your left visual field ends up in the right side of the brain, and vice versa. Fibers then synapse in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus before reaching primary visual cortex. Tap a ✂️ to cut the pathway there and see what the person loses.

Each eye sees the whole visual field.

The LGN keeps the two eyes separate: it has 6 layers, each getting input from just one eye. Its cells have center–surround receptive fields, just like ganglion cells. The eyes’ signals first combine in V1.

5Primary visual cortex: neurons that love lines

In V1, a neuron combines a row of LGN cells, so instead of a spot it responds best to a bar at one particular angle. David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel discovered this in 1959 (Nobel Prize, 1981). You’re recording from a mystery V1 neuron. Drag to rotate the bar and find its favorite angle.

6Higher-order vision: the “what” and “where” streams

From V1, visual information splits into two big streams. The ventral stream runs down into the temporal lobe and figures out what you’re looking at: color, shape, faces. The dorsal stream runs up into the parietal lobe and works out where things are and how to reach for them, including motion. Tap an area to see what the world looks like when it’s damaged.

7Putting it together: your brain fills in

What you see is the brain’s best guess, built from the signals and from what it expects. Higher areas send feedback all the way down: the LGN gets more input from cortex than from the retina. Try these.

🕳️ Find your blind spot

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no photoreceptors. Close your left eye, stare at the ✚ with your right eye, and slowly move toward or away from the screen. At about 30–50 cm the dot vanishes, and your brain fills the gap with the background.

🔺 A triangle that isn’t there

No edges are drawn, yet you see a bright white triangle on top. Neurons in V2 respond to these “illusory contours” as if the edges were real.

🔤 Context decides

The middle symbol in the row and in the column is the exact same shape. Your brain reads it as a letter or a number depending on its neighbors: top-down expectation shaping perception.

Vision recap: optics (cornea, lens) → phototransduction (rods, cones) → retinal circuit (bipolar, ganglion cells) → optic nerve, chiasm, tract → LGN of the thalamus → optic radiations → V1 (occipital lobe) → ventral “what” stream (V4, fusiform face area, inferotemporal cortex) and dorsal “where/how” stream (MT/V5, posterior parietal cortex).
