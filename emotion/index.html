The Emotional Brain

Your heart pounds, your face changes, and a feeling rushes in. Where does an emotion come from? This tour follows the neural side: the body's autonomic wiring, the theories that argue about what comes first, and the brain circuits for reward, fear, aggression and stress.

    Part 1

    An emotion has four parts, and one of them is your body

    Psychologists split an emotion into a feeling (the experience), thoughts (your appraisal of what is happening), behavior (your face, approaching or escaping) and bodily changes. The body part is run by the autonomic nervous system, which has two branches that pull in opposite directions. Switch between them.

    Every theory of emotion in the next section is really an argument about one thing: how the feeling and these body changes relate. Which comes first?

    The wiring: sympathetic neurons leave the spinal cord from its middle (thoracic and lumbar) segments; parasympathetic neurons leave from the brainstem (mostly the vagus nerve) and the bottom (sacral) segment. Most organs get both, so the two branches act like an accelerator and a brake.

    Part 2

    Which comes first, the feeling or the body?

    You meet a bear in the woods. You feel afraid and your heart races. Four classic answers disagree about the order. Pick one and press Play.

    Cannon's case against James–Lange: the organs respond too slowly (it takes seconds for your heart to change, but you feel afraid almost at once), and the bodily pattern is too similar across emotions to tell fear from anger. Schachter's answer splits the difference: the body sets how strong the feeling is, your reading of the situation picks which emotion.

    Part 3

    The facial feedback hypothesis

    If James and Lange are right that we feel what our body is doing, then your face should feed back into your feelings. Make the face, and the signal from its muscles travels back to the brain. Try the pen trick, then freeze the frown muscles with Botox.

    FrownSmile
    How you feelneutral
    Amygdala response while copying an angry face
    Move the slider. The brain sends commands out through the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII); sensations from the face come back mostly through the trigeminal nerve (V).
    Is the effect real? Mostly yes, but small. In the classic study (Strack et al., 1988), cartoons seemed funnier to people holding a pen in their teeth (forcing a smile) than in their lips. A 17-lab replication in 2016 found no effect, which made the hypothesis famous for the wrong reason. A meta-analysis of 138 studies (Coles et al., 2019) and a 19-country study (Coles et al., 2022) found a real but small effect, clearest when people deliberately pose a smile. The meter above is drawn small on purpose.
    Botox: blocking feedback. Botox paralyzes the frown muscle (corrugator). People who had it read angry sentences more slowly (Havas et al., 2010), and in an fMRI study their left amygdala responded less when they imitated angry faces (Hennenlotter et al., 2009). That fits James–Lange: take away the body's signal and the feeling weakens. The flip side: faking an emotion you don't feel at work for long periods can hurt well-being.

    Part 4

    Is there a core set of emotions?

    Darwin argued that facial expressions are universal and shared with other primates: a chimpanzee's play face looks a lot like a human laugh. On this view, emotions are motivational programs built by evolution: fear gets you away from danger, disgust keeps you away from infection. Plutchik proposed eight basic emotions in four opposing pairs. Click one to find its opposite.

    Arousal: label it, or try to lie through it

    The polygraph ("lie detector")

    The polygraph measures sympathetic arousal (breathing, sweating through skin conductance, heart rate). It detects stress, not lying, which is why a National Research Council review (2003) found it too error-prone for screening. Schachter and Singer's results have also been hard to replicate in full; the lasting idea is that arousal sets intensity and context helps pick the label.

    Part 6

    The brain's reward circuit

    In 1954, Olds and Milner found that rats would press a lever over and over to get a tiny pulse of electricity into certain brain sites. The best sites run along the medial forebrain bundle, which carries dopamine neurons from the midbrain (VTA) past the hypothalamus to the nucleus accumbens. Choose where to place the electrode, then watch the rat.

    Lever presses0

    The steeper the line, the faster the rat presses. Some rats pressed thousands of times an hour and ignored food. Humans with implanted electrodes have also reported pleasant feelings. Clinical link: depression often includes anhedonia, a loss of pleasure, and the reward circuit is one place to look for it. The lateral habenula works roughly as an "anti-reward" center: it fires when an expected reward fails to arrive, and it suppresses dopamine neurons.

    Part 7

    Lesions point to a limbic system

    Early clues came from damage. Remove an animal's cortex and it flies into rage at the slightest touch (decorticate rage), so the cortex must normally hold rage back. Papez (1937) proposed a loop of interconnected structures on the inner (medial) wall of the brain as the circuit for emotion. Press Play to follow it, then try the lesions.

    Papez's loop: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies (hypothalamus) → anterior thalamus → cingulate cortex → back to the hippocampus. We now know this loop matters more for memory than for emotion. MacLean later added the amygdala and prefrontal cortex and coined the term "limbic system". Klüver and Bucy (1939) removed both temporal lobes, including the amygdalas, in monkeys.

    Part 8

    The amygdala: fast and slow roads to fear

    Something long and curved lies on the trail. LeDoux showed that sensory information reaches the amygdala by two routes from the thalamus. The low road goes straight there: fast, crude and unconscious. The high road goes through sensory cortex first: slower, detailed and conscious. Run the race.

    Clinical link (Ch. 12): in PTSD, reminders of the trauma keep triggering the amygdala, and the prefrontal cortex has trouble calming it down. Benzodiazepines boost GABA-A receptors, which are dense in the amygdala.

    Part 10

    What makes us aggressive?

    Aggression is behavior meant to harm another. Two chemicals and one brain region keep coming up: testosterone (clear in animals, murky in humans), serotonin (which normally holds aggression back) and the ventromedial hypothalamus (which can drive an attack). Adjust them.

    Testosteronenormal
    Serotoninnormal
    Aggression

    Castrating male mice reduces fighting and testosterone replacement restores it. In humans, raising testosterone at puberty or by injection does not reliably raise aggression, though low testosterone goes with less aggression, and in women higher testosterone goes with more. The arrow runs both ways: winning raises testosterone for days, even in chess players (Mazur et al., 1992). Rhesus monkeys with the lowest serotonin activity are the most aggressive (Higley et al., 1992). Psychopathy (charming, little remorse) has a biology that is still debated.

    Part 11

    Stress runs on two pathways: fast and slow

    Stress is anything that upsets the body's balance. The brain answers in two waves. The fast one is neural: the hypothalamus fires the sympathetic nerves to the adrenal medulla, which releases epinephrine and norepinephrine within seconds. The slow one is hormonal, the HPA axis: hypothalamus (CRH) → anterior pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex → cortisol, over minutes. Cortisol then tells the hippocampus and hypothalamus to shut the system off.

    Memory trick: medulla = middle (adrenaline, fast), cortex = outer layer (cortisol, slow).

    Early life tunes the stress system

    Rat pups whose mothers lick and groom them a lot grow up with more cortisol (glucocorticoid) receptors in the hippocampus, so their brake works better (Weaver et al., 2004). Drag the slider.

    Mother's licking and groominghigh

    Acute helps, chronic harms

    Acute stress (minutes to hours) mobilizes energy, ramps up heart and lungs and briefly suppresses the immune system: built for escaping a predator. Chronic stress (weeks to years) keeps cortisol high. That suppresses immunity in a harmful way and can damage hippocampal neurons, which weakens the brake even more. Humans and baboons can stay stressed for months over social threats.

    The maternal-care effect is epigenetic: it changes how much a gene is expressed (here, the receptor gene gets methyl tags), not the gene itself. The same change shows up in the hippocampus of people with a history of childhood abuse (McGowan et al., 2009). Mild early stress followed by comfort can build resilience (stress immunization).

    Part 12

    Putting it together

    Sleep, emotion and stress feed each other. Poor sleep makes the amygdala more reactive and weakens the prefrontal brakes. Heightened emotion switches on the HPA axis. Cortisol disrupts sleep, and around it goes. That loop is part of why sleep problems, mood disorders and anxiety so often go together. Diathesis–stress model: a vulnerability (genes, early life) plus enough stress crosses a threshold into disorder.
