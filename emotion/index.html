The Emotional Brain

Your heart pounds, your face changes, and a feeling rushes in. Where does an emotion come from? This first part covers what an emotion is made of: the body's autonomic wiring, the theories that argue about what comes first, your face, and arousal.

    Part 1

    An emotion has four parts, and one of them is your body

    In plain wordsAn emotion isn't just a feeling. It also comes with thoughts, actions and body changes: your heart, lungs, gut and sweat glands all react. Two branches of your nervous system run those changes. One hits the gas (fight or flight), the other hits the brakes (rest and digest).

    Psychologists split an emotion into a feeling (the experience), thoughts (your appraisal of what is happening), behavior (your face, approaching or escaping) and bodily changes. The body part is run by the autonomic nervous system, which has two branches that pull in opposite directions. Switch between them.

    Every theory of emotion in the next section is really an argument about one thing: how the feeling and these body changes relate. Which comes first?

    The wiring: sympathetic neurons leave the spinal cord from its middle (thoracic and lumbar) segments; parasympathetic neurons leave from the brainstem (mostly the vagus nerve) and the bottom (sacral) segment. Most organs get both, so the two branches act like an accelerator and a brake.

    Part 2

    Which comes first, the feeling or the body?

    In plain wordsScientists have argued for over a century about the order of events. Do you run because you're scared, or are you scared because you notice yourself running? Each theory gives a different answer.

    You meet a bear in the woods. You feel afraid and your heart races. Four classic answers disagree about the order. Pick one and press Play.

    Cannon's case against James–Lange: the organs respond too slowly (it takes seconds for your heart to change, but you feel afraid almost at once), and the bodily pattern is too similar across emotions to tell fear from anger. Schachter's answer splits the difference: the body sets how strong the feeling is, your reading of the situation picks which emotion.

    Part 3

    The facial feedback hypothesis

    In plain wordsYour face doesn't just show your feelings. It may feed back into them a little. Smiling can nudge you to feel slightly happier, and freezing your frown with Botox may soften negative feelings. The effect is real, but small.

    If James and Lange are right that we feel what our body is doing, then your face should feed back into your feelings. Make the face, and the signal from its muscles travels back to the brain. Try the pen trick, then freeze the frown muscles with Botox.

    Move the slider. The brain sends commands out through the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII); sensations from the face come back mostly through the trigeminal nerve (V).
    Is the effect real? Mostly yes, but small. In the classic study (Strack et al., 1988), cartoons seemed funnier to people holding a pen in their teeth (forcing a smile) than in their lips. A 17-lab replication in 2016 found no effect, which made the hypothesis famous for the wrong reason. A meta-analysis of 138 studies (Coles et al., 2019) and a 19-country study (Coles et al., 2022) found a real but small effect, clearest when people deliberately pose a smile. The meter above is drawn small on purpose.
    Botox: blocking feedback. Botox paralyzes the frown muscle (corrugator). People who had it read angry sentences more slowly (Havas et al., 2010), and in an fMRI study their left amygdala responded less when they imitated angry faces (Hennenlotter et al., 2009). That fits James–Lange: take away the body's signal and the feeling weakens. The flip side: faking an emotion you don't feel at work for long periods can hurt well-being.

    Part 4

    Is there a core set of emotions?

    In plain wordsSome emotions, like fear, disgust and joy, show up on faces all over the world and even in other animals. That suggests evolution built them in to help us survive.

    Darwin argued that facial expressions are universal and shared with other primates: a chimpanzee's play face looks a lot like a human laugh. On this view, emotions are motivational programs built by evolution: fear gets you away from danger, disgust keeps you away from infection. Plutchik proposed eight basic emotions in four opposing pairs. Click one to find its opposite.

    Part 5

    Arousal: label it, or try to lie through it

    In plain wordsYour body's revved-up state (arousal) feels much the same for very different emotions, so your brain looks at the situation to work out what you're feeling. Lie detectors measure that arousal, which is why they catch nervousness, not lies.

    Two ideas that both rest on the sympathetic nervous system. In Schachter and Singer's (1962) experiment, people got a shot of epinephrine (adrenaline) or a placebo, then waited with an actor who acted either euphoric or angry. Run the experiment yourself.

    The polygraph ("lie detector")

    The polygraph measures sympathetic arousal (breathing, sweating through skin conductance, heart rate). It detects stress, not lying, which is why a National Research Council review (2003) found it too error-prone for screening. Schachter and Singer's results have also been hard to replicate in full; the lasting idea is that arousal sets intensity and context helps pick the label.
