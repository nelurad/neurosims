Agonists vs. Antagonists

Neurons talk by releasing chemical messengers called neurotransmitters. Almost every drug that affects your mind works by turning one of their receptors up or down. Here’s how.

The basics

Neurotransmitter

The messenger. One neuron releases it, it crosses a tiny gap, and it fits into a receptor on the next cell, like a key in a lock.

Agonist

A drug that turns the receptor ON. It copies the messenger, boosts the receptor, or leaves extra messenger in the gap.

Antagonist

A drug that keeps the receptor OFF. It sits in the lock or plugs the channel, without turning anything on.

Ionotropic receptor

The receptor is a gate. When the messenger binds, the gate opens and charged particles (ions) rush through.

Fast: about a thousandth of a second.

Metabotropic receptor

The receptor is a doorbell. When the messenger binds, it sends a signal inside the cell (a G protein) that sets off a chain reaction.

Slower, about a second, but the effect lasts longer.

One messenger, two receptor types: acetylcholine

The same neurotransmitter, acetylcholine, makes your bicep contract and makes your heart slow down. The difference is the receptor: the bicep uses an ionotropic receptor and the heart uses a metabotropic one. Release acetylcholine onto both and compare how fast each one responds.
