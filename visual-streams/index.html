Two Roads from the Eye

Your eyes catch light. Your brain does the seeing. A picture enters at the very back of your head as a mess of tiny edges, and as it travels forward it gets built up, piece by piece, into "my cat" and "it's right there, grab it."

    Start here

    Back of the head, then forward

    In plain wordsVision starts at the back of your brain, in V1. From there the signal splits onto two roads. The bottom road figures out what you're looking at. The top road figures out where it is and how to reach for it.
    front of the headback of the head

    Tap the stops on the brain. The brain is facing left, so the eyes would be off to the left and V1 is way over on the right.

    The "what" road (ventral stream)

    Runs down and forward into the temporal lobe.

    1. V1: tiny edges
    2. V2: outlines
    3. V4: color and curves
    4. IT cortex: whole objects and faces

    The "where/how" road (dorsal stream)

    Runs up and forward into the parietal lobe.

    1. V1: tiny edges
    2. V2 and V3
    3. MT (V5): motion
    4. Posterior parietal cortex: location and guiding your hands and eyes

    Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982) called the two roads "what" and "where" after lesion studies in monkeys. Goodale and Milner (1992) argued the top road is really about "how": using vision to guide actions. Real brains are messier than two neat roads. The streams talk to each other a lot, and signals also flow backward from higher areas. But "two roads" is a very useful first map.

    Part 1

    From edges to "a cat!"

    In plain wordsNo single neuron sees the whole cat at first. Early areas only see tiny bits. Each step forward combines the bits from the step before, so the pieces get bigger and fancier until something at the end can say "cat."
    Mess with the picture:

    The yellow square is one neuron's receptive field: the patch of the world it pays attention to. The strip under the picture is that neuron firing.

    See the white triangle? It isn't there. Nobody drew its edges. Some V2 neurons fire for edges like these anyway (von der Heydt et al., 1984).

    Try this: go to V1 and press Slide it. The neuron goes quiet, because the edge it liked moved out of its tiny patch. Now go to IT and slide, shrink or flip. The cat neuron keeps firing. Swap in a ball and it finally stops.

    This idea, that later neurons respond to the same object no matter where it is, how big it is, or which way it faces, is called invariance. Charles Gross and colleagues found IT neurons in monkeys that responded best to hands or faces (Desimone et al., 1984). Real IT neurons usually like complex shapes and object parts rather than one specific object, so our "cat neuron" is a simplification.

    Part 2

    Peek through a neuron's window

    In plain wordsEvery visual neuron looks through its own little window. In V1 the window is tiny. Farther forward, windows get huge and the neurons care about bigger, more meaningful things. Pick a brain area, then move your mouse (or finger) over the park.

    The little square shows what the neuron sees through its window.

    V1 says "tilted edge." IT says "dog!" Same picture, very different jobs. Notice that MT only gets excited when the bird flies through.

    Part 3

    MT, the motion spotter

    In plain wordsArea MT (also called V5) sits on the top road. Most of its neurons fire for movement in one direction. Let's test your MT. Some of the dots move together and the rest wander randomly. Which way is the group going?
    Dots moving together40%
    Watch the dots, then press Left or Right.

    Score: 0 / 0

    Turn the slider down until it gets hard. Most people can still feel the direction when only a small fraction of the dots move together.

    Monkeys do this same task. Single MT neurons can be about as good at it as the whole monkey (Britten et al., 1992), and small lesions in MT make the task much harder at low coherence (Newsome & Paré, 1988). The "damaged MT" switch here just makes the coherent motion weaker, to give you a feel for it.

    Part 4

    The woman who could post a letter she couldn't see

    In plain wordsA patient known as D.F. had damage to her "what" road after carbon monoxide poisoning. She couldn't say whether a slot was tilted or flat. But when asked to post a card through it, her hand turned the card just right. Her "how" road still worked.
    Turn the slot, then try both tasks a few times.

    Her results, your results

    Show me
    Post it

    Each dot is one try: ● close to the slot angle, ● sort of off, ● way off.

    So "seeing" isn't one thing. Knowing what something looks like and using vision to act on it can break separately. That's called a double dissociation.

    D.F.'s matching was close to random, while her posting was nearly normal (Goodale et al., 1991). Patients with optic ataxia, usually from damage to posterior parietal cortex, show something like the opposite: they can describe an object and its orientation but misreach and mis-grasp, especially in their peripheral vision (Perenin & Vighetto, 1988). Both patterns are simplified here.
