That Learned

LTP, LTD and the plastic brain, one synapse at a time

Everything you have ever learned, from your best friend's face to how to ride a bike, is stored as changes in synapses, the tiny junctions where neurons talk. Learning makes some of them stronger and others weaker. Zoom in on one synapse, poke it, and watch it learn.

New to this? Start with The Learning Brain (Hebb's rule, Pavlov, the sea slug). This sim zooms in on the molecules.

tap the synapse to fire it

1

the scene

One synapse, up close

A signal reaches the end of the sending neuron. It squirts a chemical messenger, glutamate, across a tiny gap onto a little bump on the receiving neuron. Little doors in the bump pop open, positive ions rush in, and the receiving neuron gets a small nudge of voltage.

The sending side is the presynaptic terminal; glutamate waits inside vesicles. The gap is the synaptic cleft. The bump is a dendritic spine. Its AMPA receptors open when glutamate binds and let Na⁺ in. The small voltage bump is an EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential). Most excitatory synapses in the brain work like this.

Look closer

Glutamate also lands on the blue NMDA receptors, but they stay shut. A magnesium ion (Mg²⁺) sits in their pore like a cork. One small EPSP is not enough to shift it. Why does that matter? Chapter 2.

2

the coincidence detector

The door that needs two keys

The NMDA receptor only lets calcium in when two things happen at the same moment: the sending neuron fired (so glutamate is stuck on it) and the receiving neuron is already excited (so the inside of the spine is less negative). The excitement pushes the magnesium cork out. Only with both keys does the door open, and calcium floods in.

At rest (about −70 mV) Mg²⁺ blocks the NMDA channel. Depolarization repels the positive Mg²⁺ out of the pore. With glutamate bound and the block removed, the channel passes Ca²⁺ (and Na⁺). Because it needs pre and post activity, the NMDA receptor is a coincidence detector: Hebb's "fire together, wire together" built into one molecule.

3

long-term potentiation

Fire together, wire together: LTP

Hit the synapse with a short, intense burst and something lasting happens. The spine stays excited, the corks pop, and a big wave of calcium rushes in. Calcium flips a molecular switch, and the spine rebuilds itself: more receptor doors, better doors, and a bigger spine. Afterwards the very same input gives a bigger response, for hours or longer. That is a memory trace in the making.

A high-frequency burst (a "tetanus", e.g. 100 pulses in 1 s) causes long-term potentiation (LTP), first shown by Bliss and Lømo (1973). Big Ca²⁺ activates CaMKII, an enzyme that can keep itself switched on by adding phosphates to itself. CaMKII phosphorylates AMPA receptors so they pass more current, and extra AMPA receptors are inserted into the membrane from stores inside the spine. The spine head enlarges. Early LTP (the first hour or so) uses proteins already present; late LTP, lasting days, needs new protein synthesis.

  2. Burst again, and again. LTP saturates: there is a ceiling.
  3. Reset, turn on APV, then burst. Test pulses still work (AMPA is fine), but no LTP.
  4. Reset, block protein synthesis, burst, then skip ahead 3 hours. Early LTP happens, then fades.

Why the spine "remembers"

The calcium is gone within seconds, but the change stays. CaMKII adds phosphates to itself, so it stays active after the calcium leaves, and the extra receptors stay put. Making the change last for days takes new proteins, made from genes switched on by the same signals.

4

long-term depression

Out of sync, lose your link: LTD

If synapses could only get stronger, they would all max out and nothing new could be stored. So synapses can also get weaker. Slow, steady, unremarkable activity lets in just a little calcium, for a long time. That calls in a different crew of enzymes, which pull receptor doors back inside. The response shrinks, and so does the spine.

Low-frequency stimulation (1 Hz for 15 minutes, as in Dudek & Bear, 1992) causes long-term depression (LTD). A modest, prolonged rise in Ca²⁺ activates phosphatases (calcineurin and PP1), which remove phosphates and trigger endocytosis of AMPA receptors. Which way a synapse goes depends on the calcium signal: a little does nothing, a moderate long rise gives LTD, a big fast rise gives LTP. This is the calcium control hypothesis (related to the BCM theory).

LTD is not "damage". It is just as much a part of learning as LTP: it clears space, sharpens which inputs matter, and is essential for some kinds of learning (for example, motor learning in the cerebellum depends on LTD). LTP and LTD also undo each other: slow stimulation after a burst can reverse LTP (called depotentiation). Try it: burst, then 1 Hz.

5

spike-timing-dependent plasticity

Timing is everything

Order matters, down to the millisecond. If the sending neuron fires just before the receiving neuron, it probably helped make it fire, so the connection gets stronger. If it fires just after, it was too late to matter, and the connection gets weaker. Hebb's rule, with a stopwatch.

This is spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) (Markram et al., 1997; Bi & Poo, 1998). Pre before post (Δt > 0): glutamate is already bound when the receiving neuron's spike travels back into the dendrite, so the Mg²⁺ cork pops and lots of Ca²⁺ enters, giving LTP. Post before pre (Δt < 0): the spike is over before glutamate arrives, so only a little Ca²⁺ gets in, giving LTD. The effect fades over a few tens of milliseconds.

6

from synapses to memories

A memory is a team of neurons

A memory is not stored in one synapse. It is a pattern: a team of neurons that were active together and, thanks to LTP, are now strongly wired to each other. Later, a small piece of the experience, like a smell, can light up one member of the team, and the strong links bring back the rest. Links that are never used weaken and can be removed.

Hebb called such a team a cell assembly; today a stored memory trace is often called an engram. Bringing back a whole pattern from part of it is pattern completion, something the hippocampus is thought to be especially good at. Weak, unused synapses can be eliminated, which is synaptic pruning (see Synaptic Pruning). This little network is a cartoon model, not a real circuit.

Tap "cinnamon". Nothing much happens: the links are weak. Now press Live it: Grandma's kitchen twice, then tap "cinnamon" again.

Things to notice

  • One experience leaves weak links. A second one makes recall from a single cue possible.
  • "Summer" belongs to both memories, so cueing it can bring back both. Shared pieces cause blending.
  • After a month or two, unused links are pruned but practised ones survive.
7

neuroplasticity, zoomed out

The brain rewires itself

LTP and LTD change how strong synapses are. Over days and weeks, the brain also changes its shape: new spines sprout and old ones vanish, and whole maps in the cortex shift towards whatever you use most. The general ability of the nervous system to change with experience is called neuroplasticity.

Structural plasticity: imaging of living mouse cortex shows that some spines appear and disappear every day, while most large spines last for months. Learning a new motor skill makes new spines grow, and some of them become stable (Xu et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2009). Map plasticity: in monkeys, training one fingertip enlarges its area in somatosensory cortex (Jenkins et al., 1990), and after a finger is lost its area is taken over by the neighbouring fingers (Merzenich et al., 1984).

8

a worked example

Riding a bike vs. learning a capital

So far everything has happened in one synapse or a cartoon network. Here is how it plays out when you actually learn something. Pick a memory, learning to ride a bike or learning that the capital of Australia is Canberra, then step through time: the first try, the first hour, that night, weeks later, years later. Watch which parts of the brain are busy, and what happens to two key synapses.

Riding a bike is a procedural memory ("knowing how"). It is built slowly by practice, mostly in motor cortex, the cerebellum and the basal ganglia. A capital city is a declarative (more exactly, semantic) memory ("knowing that"). The hippocampus can grab it in one go and then slowly hand it over to the cortex, a process called systems consolidation. Both run on the same NMDA, Ca²⁺, LTP and LTD machinery from chapters 2 to 5. What differs is where it happens, how often it is repeated, and what acts as the teacher.

9

the big picture

What learning looks like in the brain

1. Glutamate talks

Glutamate opens AMPA receptors: Na⁺ in, a small EPSP. NMDA receptors stay corked by Mg²⁺.

2. NMDA listens for teamwork

Glutamate + depolarization together pop the Mg²⁺ cork. Ca²⁺ enters. That is the "learn now" signal.

3. Lots of Ca²⁺ = LTP

Big, fast Ca²⁺ → CaMKII → more and better AMPA receptors, bigger spine. Lasting needs new proteins.

4. A little Ca²⁺ = LTD

Modest, long Ca²⁺ → phosphatases → AMPA receptors removed, smaller spine. Timing (STDP) sets the direction too.

Put together: experiences that make neurons fire together strengthen the links between them (LTP), links that do not fit get weaker (LTD), unused ones are pruned, and over weeks spines and whole maps reshape. That is what learning looks like in the brain.
