The Learning Brain

Every time you learn something, your brain physically changes. Synapses get stronger or weaker, new connections sprout, and dendrites grow. This tour follows learning from a sea slug's reflex to the receptor that acts like Hebb's rule in a single molecule.

    Part 1

    Learning vs. memory, and the kinds of "knowing how"

    Learning is acquiring new information or behavior, so that behavior changes with experience. Memory is storing and retrieving it. They are two sides of one process: we only know learning happened because something was remembered. Most of this page is about nondeclarative learning, which shows up in performance rather than in something you can state. Click a type to see which brain regions it relies on.

    Part 2

    Learning at one synapse: the sea slug Aplysia

    Eric Kandel picked Aplysia because its nervous system has few neurons, and they are large and identifiable. A touch to the siphon excites a sensory neuron, which excites a motor neuron, and the gill withdraws. Two simple, nonassociative forms of learning (only one stimulus is involved) change this reflex. Try them.

    Gill withdrawal on each siphon touch–
    Transmitter released by the sensory neuronnormal
    Touch the siphon several times in a row.

    Habituation: the sensory neuron releases less glutamate with each harmless touch (Castellucci et al., 1970). Sensitization: a tail shock excites facilitating interneurons that release serotonin onto the sensory neuron's terminal; through cAMP and protein kinase A, the terminal releases more transmitter, so any touch now gives a bigger withdrawal. With repeated training, both changes can last days, and long-term sensitization even grows new synaptic terminals. Kandel shared the 2000 Nobel Prize for this work.

    Part 3

    Skills and priming: learning without knowing it

    Skill learning is getting better at a task through practice: motor skills (mirror tracing), perceptual skills (reading mirror-reversed text) and cognitive skills (puzzles). It relies on the basal ganglia (striatum), cerebellum and motor cortex, and is impaired in Parkinson's and Huntington's disease, which damage the basal ganglia. Patient H.M. improved at mirror tracing across days while insisting each day that he had never tried it.

    Mirror tracing over three days

    Try priming on yourself

    Priming is a change in how you process a stimulus because you saw it (or something like it) earlier. Step 1: read the words. Step 2: complete each word stem with the first word that comes to mind.

    Priming survives amnesia: amnesic patients complete stems with studied words as often as anyone, even though they can't recall the list. Perceptual priming is linked to reduced activity in occipitotemporal (visual) cortex on the repeat: the brain does the same job with less effort.

    Part 4

    Classical (Pavlovian) conditioning

    Associative learning links two events. In Pavlov's experiments, food in a dog's mouth (an unconditioned stimulus, US) triggers salivation (an unconditioned response, UR). A bell starts out meaningless. Ring the bell just before food, again and again, and the bell becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that triggers salivation by itself, now a conditioned response (CR). Run the experiment, then extinguish it.

    Salivation on each trial
    Start by ringing the bell alone: before conditioning, it does nothing.

    Extinction does not erase the learning. After a rest, the response partly comes back on its own (spontaneous recovery), which shows that extinction is new learning layered on top. That is the logic behind exposure therapy for phobias and PTSD. (And yes, Pavlov did use bells, along with metronomes and buzzers.)

    Part 5

    Eyeblink conditioning: a memory stored in the cerebellum

    A tone is followed by a puff of air to the eye. At first you blink only after the puff (the reflex). After many pairings you blink before it, timed to close the eye just as the puff arrives. Richard Thompson's lab traced this memory to the cerebellum: the tone arrives over mossy fibers, the puff over climbing fibers from the inferior olive, and the two converge on Purkinje cells and the deep interpositus nucleus.

    Eyelid on the last trial
    Run a few pairings and watch a blink appear before the puff.

    A small lesion of the interpositus nucleus abolishes the learned blink but leaves the reflex blink to the puff intact (McCormick & Thompson, 1984), so the learned part is stored in the cerebellum, not in the reflex pathway. In humans, PET showed cerebellar activity during eyeblink conditioning (Logan & Grafton, 1995), and people with damage to one side of the cerebellum learn the conditioned blink only with the eye on the intact side (Papka et al., 1994).

    Part 6

    Instrumental (operant) conditioning

    In instrumental conditioning, an animal learns the link between its own behavior and a consequence. In a Skinner box, a rat learns that pressing a lever delivers a food pellet. How the rewards are scheduled shapes how it responds. Choose a schedule and watch the cumulative record (the steeper the line, the faster the pressing).

    Cumulative lever presses0 presses · 0 pellets

    Instrumental learning taps many brain regions at once. The striatum and its dopamine input are central: dopamine neurons fire to unexpected rewards, which helps stamp in actions that pay off, and with long practice behavior shifts from goal-directed to habit (dorsal striatum). Prefrontal cortex, amygdala and the reward circuit also contribute. Ticks on the record mark pellets.

    Part 7

    Memory storage requires physical changes at synapses

    Charles Sherrington, who named the synapse, guessed in 1897 that changing synapses could be the basis of learning. There are several ways to make a synapse more effective, and some are physiological (how much is released, how sensitive the receiver is) while others are structural (bigger or brand-new synapses).     Part 8

    Varied experience makes the brain grow

    In the 1960s, Mark Rosenzweig, Marian Diamond and colleagues raised rats in three kinds of housing: alone in a bare cage (impoverished, IC), in small groups (standard, SC), or in large groups with toys, ladders and wheels that changed daily (enriched, EC). Enriched rats had measurably different brains. Pick a condition.

Each grown neuron is random, so counts vary from cell to cell, just as in real tissue.

      Part 9

      Hebb's rule: cells that fire together wire together

      Donald Hebb (1949) proposed that when neuron A repeatedly helps make neuron B fire, the A→B synapse gets stronger. Synapses that don't contribute weaken. A memory could then be a pattern of strengthened connections. Below, a strong input (food) always fires the cell; a weak input (bell) cannot, at first.

      This is classical conditioning built from one Hebbian synapse. Do real synapses follow Hebb's rule? Long-term potentiation says yes.

      Part 10

      Long-term potentiation in the hippocampus

      In 1973, Bliss and Lømo stimulated the perforant path in the hippocampus of anesthetized rabbits. A brief, high-frequency burst (a tetanus) made every later test pulse produce a bigger response, and the increase lasted for hours. This lasting strengthening is long-term potentiation (LTP).       Part 11

      NMDA and AMPA receptors team up in LTP

      At CA1 synapses, glutamate acts on two receptor types. AMPA receptors open whenever glutamate binds and let Na⁺ in, depolarizing the spine. NMDA receptors also bind glutamate, but at rest their channel is plugged by a magnesium ion (Mg²⁺). Only when the spine is already depolarized does the Mg²⁺ get pushed out, letting Ca²⁺ flood in. That makes the NMDA receptor a coincidence detector: Hebb's rule in one molecule. Set the two conditions.

      The unblocking curve uses the measured voltage dependence of the Mg²⁺ block (Jahr & Stevens, 1990) at 1 mM Mg²⁺. Steps of LTP: (1) Mg²⁺ blocks NMDA at rest, so weak input acts through AMPA only; (2) strong, repeated input depolarizes the spine and expels Mg²⁺; (3) Ca²⁺ enters and activates CaMKII and PKC, which make existing AMPA receptors conduct better and insert new ones; (4) a retrograde messenger (possibly nitric oxide) tells the presynaptic terminal to release more glutamate; (5) CREB turns on genes for new proteins, which make late LTP last. LTD: prolonged slow stimulation lets in a little Ca²⁺, which activates phosphatases that remove AMPA receptors.

      Part 12

      Is LTP a mechanism of memory?

      LTP has the right properties: it forms fast, lasts long and occurs where memories form. The strongest test: block it and see if learning fails. In the Morris water maze, a rat swims in murky water to find a hidden platform using room cues. Compare a normal rat with one given APV, which blocks hippocampal NMDA receptors (Morris et al., 1986).
