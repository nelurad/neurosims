Memory in Real Life

Memory isn't a video camera. It bends with the questions we're asked, sharpens when emotions run high, and sometimes works too well or not well enough. This part covers false memories, emotional memory and memory disorders.

    Part 1

    Remembering is rebuilding

    In plain wordsRemembering isn't like replaying a video. Your brain rebuilds the memory every time, and it can slip in details that never happened, while you feel totally sure.

    Forgetting is often interference, not just fading: old learning disrupts new (proactive) and new learning disrupts old (retroactive). And every time you recall a memory it becomes unstable again and must be stored again (reconsolidation), so it can be strengthened, weakened or changed.

    The wording changes the memory

    Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed people a film of a car crash, then asked: "About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?" Pick the verb.

    Reconsolidation

    Recall reopens the memory

    In rats, blocking protein synthesis in the amygdala right after a fear memory was recalled erased the memory, even though it was old and well consolidated (Nader et al., 2000). This window is a target for PTSD treatment research.

    Eyewitnesses

    Confident is not accurate

    Leading questions, later information and retelling all reshape memories. That is why eyewitness memory is unreliable, even when the witness is sure.

    Study tip

    Use it for your exam

    Test yourself (retrieval practice beats rereading), space your study over days, sleep after studying, and connect new ideas to what you know so there are more routes back to them.

    Part 2

    Why emotional events stick

    In plain wordsScary, exciting or embarrassing moments stick better. The amygdala, the brain's alarm system, tells the hippocampus this one matters, save it well.

    An emotional event activates the amygdala and releases stress hormones: epinephrine from the adrenal medulla and cortisol from the adrenal cortex. The amygdala then boosts consolidation in the hippocampus, so the memory comes out vivid and long-lasting. Block the hormone signal and the boost disappears.

    Flashbulb memories

    Memories of shocking news feel vivid and certain. When researchers tested people the day after 9/11 and again months later, details changed as much as for ordinary memories, but people's confidence stayed high (Talarico & Rubin, 2003). Curves are illustrative.

    Part 3

    When memory goes wrong (Ch. 12)

    In plain wordsMany mental health conditions involve memory systems that work too strongly, too weakly or get stuck in a loop.

    Several psychological disorders can be understood partly as memory systems working too well, not well enough, or in a loop.

    PTSD

    A memory that won't fade

    Flashbacks and intrusive memories: an over-consolidated emotional memory plus fear conditioning that won't extinguish. The hippocampus is smaller in PTSD; twin studies suggest this may be a pre-existing risk factor. Exposure therapy works through extinction, which is new learning.

    Depression

    A negative bias

    Negative memories come to mind easily, and rumination replays and strengthens them. An overactive stress (HPA) axis and prolonged cortisol can harm the hippocampus. ECT helps many patients but causes retrograde amnesia for recent memories.

    Schizophrenia

    The LTP receptor

    Memory and working-memory problems and underactive frontal lobes. PCP and ketamine block NMDA receptors and cause schizophrenia-like symptoms, leading to the glutamate hypothesis. That is the same receptor that drives LTP.

    OCD and Tourette's

    Habit loops stuck on

    The basal ganglia support habit learning. In OCD (with orbitofrontal cortex) and Tourette's syndrome (tics), these loops misfire and behaviors repeat without control.
