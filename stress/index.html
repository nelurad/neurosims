Stress and the HPA Axis

A test tomorrow, a near-miss on the road, a fight with a friend: your body answers all of them with the same two-wave stress response. Learn how the fast adrenaline wave and the slow cortisol wave work, how the system shuts itself off, and why stress that never ends causes harm.

    What is stress, really?

    In plain wordsStress is your body's response to anything that threatens its balance: a test, a fight, a scary noise, not enough sleep. The thing causing it is the stressor. Your body's reaction is the stress response. A little helps you rise to the moment. A lot, for too long, wears you down.
    1

    The brain spots a threat

    Your amygdala sounds the alarm, and the hypothalamus, the brain's control center for your body, gets the stress response moving.

    Like realizing the test you forgot about starts in five minutes.
    2

    Fast wave: adrenaline

    Within seconds, nerves tell the adrenal glands (on top of your kidneys) to release adrenaline. Your heart pounds, you breathe faster and energy floods in.

    That jolt you feel when you almost miss a step on the stairs.
    3

    Slow wave: cortisol

    Over minutes, a chain of hormones (the HPA axis) ends with the adrenal glands releasing cortisol. It keeps energy up, and then helps switch the whole response off.

    Cortisol is why you still feel wound up long after the scare is over.

    Words you'll see a lot

    StressorAnything that throws your body off balance, from a predator to a deadline.
    HomeostasisYour body's steady, balanced state. Stress pushes you away from it.
    Sympathetic nervous systemThe "fight or flight" branch that revs the body up.
    Adrenal glandsSmall glands on top of the kidneys. Middle = medulla, outer layer = cortex.
    Adrenaline (epinephrine)The fast stress hormone from the adrenal medulla.
    CortisolThe slow stress hormone from the adrenal cortex.
    HPA axisHypothalamus → Pituitary → Adrenal cortex: the hormone chain that makes cortisol.
    Negative feedbackWhen the end product (cortisol) tells the system to slow down, like a thermostat.

    Part 1

    Stress runs on two pathways: fast and slow

    In plain wordsYour body answers stress in two waves. The fast wave uses nerves and adrenaline and takes seconds. The slow wave uses a chain of hormones ending in cortisol and takes minutes. When the danger passes, cortisol itself tells the brain to switch the system off.

    The fast wave is neural: the hypothalamus fires the sympathetic nerves to the adrenal medulla, which releases epinephrine and norepinephrine within seconds. The slow wave is hormonal, the HPA axis: hypothalamus (CRH) → anterior pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex → cortisol, over minutes. Cortisol then tells the hippocampus and hypothalamus to shut the system off. Press Stressor!, then try chronic stress.

    Memory trick: medulla = middle (adrenaline, fast), cortex = outer layer (cortisol, slow).

    Cortisol usually peaks roughly 20 to 40 minutes after a stressor begins (Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004). The hippocampus is rich in cortisol (glucocorticoid) receptors, which is why it acts as one of the main brakes on the HPA axis, and why it is vulnerable when cortisol stays high.

    Part 2

    Acute stress helps, chronic stress harms

    In plain wordsA short burst of stress helps you: more energy, sharper focus, faster reactions. The problem is stress that never switches off. Weeks of high cortisol wear down the body, and even the brain.

    Acute stress (minutes to hours) mobilizes energy, ramps up the heart and lungs and briefly damps the immune system: built for escaping a predator. Chronic stress (weeks to years) keeps cortisol high. Humans and baboons can stay stressed for months over social threats alone. Here is what cortisol does around the body.

    Energy

    Short burst: raises blood sugar so your muscles and brain have fuel to act.

    Weeks on end: keeps blood sugar high, linked to belly fat and a higher risk of type 2 diabetes.

    Immune system

    Short burst: briefly damps inflammation.

    Weeks on end: suppresses immune defenses, so wounds heal more slowly and infections take hold more easily.

    Memory

    Short burst: a moderate rise helps lock in memories of the event.

    Weeks on end: can shrink dendrites in the hippocampus and hurt memory, which also weakens the HPA brake.

    Sleep and mood

    Short burst: keeps you alert and focused.

    Weeks on end: disrupts sleep and raises the risk of depression and anxiety.

    See it in the simulation in Part 1: switch on Chronic stress and watch hippocampus health fall and cortisol climb as the brake weakens.

    Part 3

    Early life tunes the stress system

    In plain wordsHow you're cared for as a baby can tune your stress system for life. In rats, pups with attentive mothers grow up calmer under stress, because care changes how their genes are used, not the genes themselves.

    Rat pups whose mothers lick and groom them a lot grow up with more cortisol (glucocorticoid) receptors in the hippocampus, so their brake works better (Weaver et al., 2004). Drag the slider.

    Mother's licking and groominghigh

    Changing the volume, not the song

    The maternal-care effect is epigenetic: it changes how much a gene is expressed (here, the receptor gene gets chemical methyl tags that turn it down), not the DNA sequence itself.

    The same change shows up in the hippocampus of people with a history of childhood abuse (McGowan et al., 2009). Mild early stress followed by comfort, on the other hand, can build resilience (stress immunization).

    Part 4

    The stress, sleep and emotion loop

    In plain wordsStress, sleep and emotion feed each other. Stress hurts sleep, bad sleep makes emotions bigger, and big emotions trigger more stress. Breaking any one link can help break the whole loop.

    Poor sleep makes the amygdala more reactive and weakens the prefrontal brakes (see Emotion circuits). Heightened emotion switches on the HPA axis. Cortisol disrupts sleep, and around it goes. That loop is part of why sleep problems, mood disorders and anxiety so often go together.

    The diathesis–stress model

    Why does the same stress lead to a disorder in one person and not another? A vulnerability (the diathesis: genes, early life) plus enough stress crosses a threshold into disorder. The more vulnerable you are, the less stress it takes.

    This is why early-life care (Part 3) matters so much: it sets how big your stress response is for the rest of your life.
