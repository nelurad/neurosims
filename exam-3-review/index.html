Unit1

Sensation & Perception

receptors → maps → eyes → ears → a brain that fills in the blanks

1.1From stimulus to signal

Transduction is the first step of every sense: a receptor cell turns some kind of energy (light, pressure, sound waves, chemicals) into an electrical change called a graded receptor potential. Graded means its size follows the strength of the stimulus.

Most receptors depolarize when stimulated. Photoreceptors are the odd ones out: in the dark they sit depolarized and keep releasing glutamate, and light makes them hyperpolarize, so they release less.

Labeled lines: every action potential looks the same, so the brain knows what kind of sensation it is from which pathway carries it. Press gently on a closed eyelid and you "see" light, because the optic nerve can only report vision.

Intensity is coded by rate: a stronger stimulus gives a bigger receptor potential, which makes the sensory neuron fire more action potentials per second (and recruits more neurons). The spikes themselves never get taller or travel faster.

1.2Maps of the body: receptive fields and the homunculus

A sensory neuron's receptive field is the patch of the world (a piece of skin, a spot in the visual field) that changes its firing. Where receptors are packed tightly, receptive fields are small, so you can tell two nearby touches apart. That is what the two-point discrimination test measures.

The cortex keeps topographic maps: neighbours on the body (or retina, or cochlea) are handled by neighbouring cortex. There's a somatotopic map in somatosensory cortex (the homunculus), a retinotopic map in visual cortex and a tonotopic map in auditory cortex. The maps are distorted: body parts with more receptors get more cortex, not body parts that are bigger.

1.3The retina: rods, cones and the circuit

Light passes through the retina to the photoreceptors at the back. The signal then runs the other way: photoreceptor → bipolar cell → ganglion cell. Ganglion cell axons bundle together to form the optic nerve. Ganglion cells are the first cells in the chain to fire action potentials.

Rods (about 90 million or more) work in dim light. Many rods converge onto one ganglion cell, so weak signals add up: great sensitivity, poor detail, and no colour, since there's only one kind of rod pigment.

Cones (about 6 million) need brighter light, come in three types for colour, and are packed in the fovea with little convergence, which gives sharp detail.

Dark adaptation is slow: cones recover in about 10 minutes, but rods need 20–30 minutes to rebuild their pigment, which is why a dark room slowly "appears".

Why more men are colour-blind The genes for the red- and green-sensitive cone pigments sit on the X chromosome. Men (XY) have one X, so one faulty copy is enough. Women (XX) usually have a working copy on the other X, so they're mostly carriers. Red-green colour blindness affects about 1 in 12 men but only about 1 in 200 women.

1.4The visual pathway: where a lesion cuts the view

Each eye sees both halves of the visual field. At the optic chiasm, fibres from the nasal half of each retina (the half nearest your nose) cross over; fibres from the temporal half stay on their own side. The result: after the chiasm, each optic tract carries the opposite half of the visual field from both eyes. The left hemisphere sees the right visual field, and vice versa. Tracts go to the thalamus (LGN) and then to primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe.

Optic nerveBefore the crossing. One eye goes completely blind.

Optic tractAfter the crossing. The same side of the world is lost in both eyes, opposite to the lesion: left tract → right visual field gone.

1.5Two streams out of the occipital lobe

From V1, visual information splits into two broad routes.

The dorsal stream runs up into the parietal lobe: the "where/how" pathway for location, motion and guiding actions. Damage can cause optic ataxia: the person can see and name an object but can't guide their hand to it accurately.

The ventral stream runs down into the temporal lobe: the "what" pathway for recognising objects, colours and faces. Damage can cause visual agnosia: the person sees fine but can't recognise things by sight. The fusiform face area on the underside of the temporal lobe is specialised for faces, and damage there causes prosopagnosia (face blindness).

1.6Hearing: the cochlea's piano

Sound waves move the eardrum and middle-ear bones, which push fluid inside the snail-shaped cochlea. That ripples the basilar membrane. On it sit hair cells, the auditory receptors: when their tiny hairs (stereocilia) bend, ion channels open and the cell depolarizes. That's transduction for hearing.

The membrane is narrow and stiff at the base (near the middle ear) and wide and floppy at the apex. So high pitches vibrate the base and low pitches vibrate the apex. This place code is kept all the way up to auditory cortex as a tonotopic map.

1.8Perception is built, not copied

The brain doesn't just record; it constructs a best guess. Bottom-up processing builds up from the raw sensory input. Top-down processing uses expectations, context and knowledge to interpret it.

Ambiguous (bistable) figures show this clearly: the image on your retina stays exactly the same, but your perception flips between two interpretations. The change happens in the brain, not the eye.

Unit2

Attention & Executive Function

the spotlight, what slips past it, and the frontal lobe at the controls

2.1The spotlight of attention

You can't process everything at once, so attention selects. Selective attention means boosting one stream of information while turning down the rest. Picture following one friend's voice on a crowded bus while other conversations become background mush. The ignored stream is still monitored a little: hear your own name and attention snaps over.

Two forces move the spotlight:

Bottom-up (exogenous): something in the world grabs attention automatically: a flash, a sudden movement, a loud bang. It is fast and hard to resist.

Top-down (endogenous): you steer attention on purpose, guided by goals, such as scanning a crowd for someone in a yellow raincoat. It is slower and effortful, and the prefrontal and parietal cortex direct it.

2.2What you don't see

Inattentional blindness: missing something fully visible because attention is busy elsewhere. In the classic study by Simons and Chabris (1999), about half of viewers counting basketball passes never noticed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the game.

Change blindness: failing to notice that something has changed between one view and the next, especially when the change happens during a blink, a cut or a flicker. We keep far less detail than it feels like we do.

2.3The prefrontal cortex: executive control

TapWho does what up front?

Tap a region of the frontal lobe.

Executive functions are the brain's management team, centred on the prefrontal cortex (PFC):

Working memory: holding and working with a few items for seconds (keeping a recipe step in mind as you measure).
Inhibitory control: stopping a strong automatic response.
Task switching: shifting between rules or goals.
Planning: organising steps toward a goal.

The Stroop effect shows inhibitory control at work: reading is automatic, so naming the ink colour of a colour word is slower when the word names a different colour. The PFC (with the anterior cingulate) has to override the habit.

ADHD is best understood as a difficulty with these executive functions, especially inhibiting impulses and sustaining attention. It involves frontal-striatal circuits and their dopamine and noradrenaline signalling.

Phineas Gage (1848)An iron rod shot through the front of his skull, damaging the orbitofrontal / ventromedial PFC. He survived, walked and talked, and his memory and intelligence seemed intact, but friends said he was "no longer Gage": impulsive, rude, poor at planning, and bad at judging what was socially appropriate. His case linked this region to personality, judgement and social behaviour.
Working memory vs long-term memoryWorking memory is the mental scratchpad: small (a handful of items), brief (seconds unless rehearsed), active, and dependent on the prefrontal cortex. Long-term memory is the archive: huge, lasting, and for new facts and events it depends on the hippocampus to be formed. Losing one doesn't mean losing the other.

Unit3

Learning & Memory

kinds of memory, how they form, how they fail, and the synapse that learns

3.1Memory isn't one thing

Declarative ("knowing that") memory can be consciously recalled and put into words. It splits into episodic memory, for personal events tied to a time and place, and semantic memory, for facts and general knowledge. Forming new declarative memories depends on the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe.

Nondeclarative ("knowing how") memory shows up in performance, not recollection. It includes procedural skills and habits (basal ganglia, cerebellum), priming (an earlier exposure makes later processing faster, cortex), and classical conditioning (cerebellum for timed reflexes, amygdala for learned fear). You can have a skill you can do perfectly but can't explain, like keeping your balance on a bike.

3.2H.M. and amnesia

In 1953 Henry Molaison (H.M.) had the medial temporal lobes removed on both sides, including most of the hippocampus, to stop severe seizures. Afterwards he could hold a conversation and his IQ was normal, but he could no longer form new declarative memories: he met researchers for decades and never remembered them. That's anterograde amnesia.

He also lost some memories from the years just before surgery (a temporally graded retrograde amnesia) but kept older childhood memories. And on a mirror-drawing task he improved day after day while insisting he'd never done it: procedural memory survived, so it must not depend on the hippocampus.

3.3From experience to recall: where memory can break

Encoding turns experience into a memory trace and needs attention. Consolidation stabilises the trace over hours to years. New memories are fragile until then, which is why a concussion can wipe out the last few minutes. Storage keeps it, spread across cortex. Retrieval brings it back, helped by good cues.

Reconstructive memory: recall isn't playing back a recording. Each time, the brain rebuilds the memory from pieces and fills gaps with expectations.

Elizabeth Loftus showed the misinformation effect: information given after an event, such as a leading question, can be woven into the memory and then feel just as real as the original. This matters for eyewitness testimony.

Emotion strengthens memory. During arousing events, stress hormones and the amygdala boost consolidation in the hippocampus, so emotional memories are stronger and more vivid. Flashbulb memories feel especially vivid and certain, but confidence isn't accuracy: their details drift like any other memory.

3.4Conditioning has its own circuits

In eyeblink conditioning, a tone (CS) is followed by a puff of air (US) aimed at the eye. After pairings, the tone alone triggers a well-timed blink (CR). Richard Thompson's work in rabbits showed this learning depends on the cerebellum: with a cerebellar lesion, the animal still blinks to the puff (that reflex is intact) but never learns to blink to the tone.

Fear conditioning works the same way with a different hub: a tone paired with a mild shock comes to trigger freezing and a racing heart. That needs the amygdala.

TrainPair, then test

Pick a kind of conditioning. Pair the tone with the US a few times, then test the tone alone. Then try it with a lesion.

3.5Fire together, wire together: LTP

Donald Hebb proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps fire another, the connection between them strengthens. Long-term potentiation (LTP) is the lasting strengthening of a synapse after strong, coincident activity. It is the best-studied cellular basis of learning, especially in the hippocampus.

The transmitter is glutamate. AMPA receptors open whenever glutamate binds and let Na⁺ in. NMDA receptors are a coincidence detector: they need glutamate and a depolarized postsynaptic cell, because at rest a Mg²⁺ ion plugs the channel. When both happen, Mg²⁺ is pushed out and Ca²⁺ floods in.

Calcium triggers the changes: more AMPA receptors inserted, bigger dendritic spines, and, via signals back to the presynaptic side, more transmitter release. Neurons that are active together for the same experience end up strongly linked, and that linked network is the association.

ToggleThe two keys of the NMDA receptor

Turn on the presynaptic input, the postsynaptic depolarization, or both. Repeat the pairing to strengthen the synapse.

Unit4

Emotion

which comes first, the fast road to fear, the brakes, and the reward signal

4.1Four theories, four orders of events

Imagine a sudden crack of thunder right overhead. Your heart jumps and you feel afraid. The theories disagree about the order:

James-Lange: the bodily arousal comes first, and the emotion is your perception of it ("I'm trembling, so I'm afraid"). So if you block the body's arousal, the emotion should weaken.
Cannon-Bard: the thalamus sends the signal to the cortex (feeling) and to the body (arousal) at the same time; neither causes the other. Cannon argued that bodily changes are too slow and too similar across emotions to explain the feeling.
Schachter-Singer two-factor: emotion = arousal + a cognitive label taken from the situation. One pounding pulse can end up labelled fear, anger or excitement. If you blame the wrong source, that's misattribution of arousal.
Facial feedback: your facial expression feeds back to shape the feeling. Holding a smile can nudge you a little toward feeling happier, though the effect is small and debated.

Mix-up alert: James-Lange vs Cannon-Bard vs Schachter-Singer

James-LangeBody first, then feeling. Arousal causes the emotion. No arousal, weaker emotion.
Cannon-BardBody and feeling together, both triggered by the thalamus. Independent of each other.
Schachter-SingerArousal plus a label. Arousal is general; the thinking about why you're aroused picks the emotion.

Hook: James-Lange is a line (body → feeling). Cannon-Bard is a fork (thalamus → both). Schachter-Singer has a sign-post (arousal + label).

4.2The amygdala: the brain's alarm

Joseph LeDoux traced two routes from the senses to the amygdala. The low road goes thalamus → amygdala directly: quick and crude, so your body startles before you know what you saw. The high road goes thalamus → sensory cortex → amygdala: slower but detailed, and it can confirm the danger or call off the alarm ("it's just a coiled rope").

Too little amygdala matters too. In Klüver-Bucy syndrome, monkeys with both temporal lobes (including the amygdalae) removed became tame and fearless. Psychopathy is linked to a hypoactive amygdala: weak fear and poor reading of other people's fear and distress.

Aggression itself is generated below the cortex, in the hypothalamus and brainstem. The cortex acts as a brake: disconnect it and animals show sham rage, fierce attack displays at trivial triggers, aimed at nothing.

4.3Reward: dopamine and prediction error

Dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) project to the nucleus accumbens (and on to the PFC). This is the core reward pathway.

Wolfram Schultz found that these neurons don't simply signal "reward". They signal reward prediction error: the difference between what you got and what you expected.

Better than expected (an unexpected reward): a burst of firing.
Exactly as expected: no change at the reward; the burst moves to the cue that predicts it.
Worse than expected (a predicted reward is omitted): a dip below baseline, right when the reward should have come.

That error signal is a teaching signal: it tells the brain to update its predictions. It's also why unpredictable rewards are so compelling.

Unit5

Stress

a fast system, a slow system, and why switching off matters

5.1Two stress systems, two speeds

The SAM axis (sympathetic–adrenal–medullary) is the fast system. The hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system, which signals the adrenal medulla (the inner part of the adrenal gland) to release epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine. Within seconds: racing heart, faster breathing, glucose released, pupils wide.

The HPA axis (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal) is the slow hormonal chain. The hypothalamus releases CRH, which tells the anterior pituitary to release ACTH into the blood, which tells the adrenal cortex (the outer part) to release cortisol. Cortisol peaks after minutes (about 15–30) and acts for hours. It raises blood glucose, and it turns down "non-urgent" jobs: immune function, digestion, growth and reproduction. Cortisol then switches its own release off through negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary (and the hippocampus helps).

Sapolsky's zebrasRobert Sapolsky asked why zebras don't get ulcers. A zebra's stress response is intense but short: sprint, escape, and minutes later it's grazing with the response switched off. Humans can switch it on just by thinking about a mortgage, a deadline or an argument, and keep it on for weeks.
Acute helps, chronic harmsShort bursts mobilise energy and sharpen focus; that is adaptive. Prolonged high cortisol suppresses the immune system, raises blood pressure, disrupts sleep, digestion and reproduction, and can damage the hippocampus, which then gives weaker negative feedback, so the loop keeps itself running.

Unit6

Sleep & Biological Rhythms

the body clock, the night's architecture, and what sleep is for

6.1Three kinds of rhythm

Circadian (circa diem, "about a day"): roughly 24-hour cycles, like the sleep-wake cycle, body temperature, cortisol and melatonin.

Ultradian: shorter than a day and repeating several times in 24 hours, like the ~90-minute sleep cycle, hunger pangs or a newborn's feeding schedule.

Infradian: longer than a day, like the ~28-day menstrual cycle. Circannual rhythms (about a year, such as seasonal migration or breeding) are a kind of infradian rhythm.

SortHow long is the cycle?

Drag (or tap) each rhythm into its bin.

Mix-up alert: circadian vs ultradian vs infradian

Ultradian < 24 hThink "ultra-frequent": happens many times a day.
Circadian ≈ 24 hAbout one day.
Infradian > 24 h"Infrequent": less than once a day (monthly, yearly).

Watch out: the prefixes feel backwards. Ultra refers to how often the rhythm happens (very often), so the cycle is short.

6.2The master clock and how light sets it

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, sitting right above the optic chiasm, is the master clock. Its cells keep time with a molecular loop of clock genes. Cut off from all cues, the clock free-runs with a period a bit longer than a day (about 24.2 hours in careful lab studies; older studies found closer to 25), so sleep drifts later and later. Cues that reset it each day are zeitgebers ("time-givers"). Light is the strongest; meals, exercise and social schedules help too.

Light reaches the clock through a special third type of photoreceptor: intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) that contain melanopsin and respond best to blue light. Their axons form the retinohypothalamic tract to the SCN. The SCN then controls the pineal gland, which releases melatonin in darkness. Because this route is separate from rods and cones, damage to ipRGCs disrupts entrainment of the clock, not ordinary sight.

Teen body clocks run lateDuring adolescence the circadian rhythm undergoes a phase delay: melatonin rises later at night, so teens naturally feel sleepy later and want to wake later. It's biology, not just habit, and it eases in the early twenties.

6.3A night of sleep, stage by stage

EEG reveals the stages. Awake: fast, small beta waves when alert, alpha when relaxed with eyes closed. N1: drowsy, theta waves. N2: sleep spindles (brief bursts) and K-complexes (big single waves). Spindles help protect sleep from disturbance and are linked to memory consolidation. N3: slow-wave sleep, large slow delta waves, the deepest stage and the hardest to wake from. REM: the EEG looks fast and desynchronized, almost like waking, with rapid eye movements and vivid dreams, while the brainstem actively inhibits motor neurons, causing muscle atonia so we don't act out our dreams.

A cycle lasts about 90 minutes, repeated 4–6 times a night. Slow-wave sleep is concentrated early in the night; REM periods get longer toward morning. So cutting a night short mostly costs REM.

Slow-wave (N3)Big, slow, synchronized delta waves. Deepest, hardest to wake. Muscles have some tone. Mostly in the first half of the night.
vs

6.4What sleep is for, and when it goes wrong

Taking out the trashDuring sleep, the spaces between brain cells widen and cerebrospinal fluid flushes through: the glymphatic system. In mice, waste such as beta-amyloid (the protein that builds up in Alzheimer's disease) is cleared much faster during sleep.
Filing the day's memoriesIn deep sleep, the hippocampus replays patterns from the day, helping move memories into longer-term cortical storage (consolidation). Spindles and slow waves are timed with this replay.
Building the brainNewborns sleep 14–17 hours a day, and about half of it is REM. The amount of sleep, especially REM, is highest when the brain is developing fastest, which suggests sleep helps wire it.
Keeping emotions in checkYoo and colleagues (2007) found that after a night without sleep, the amygdala reacted about 60% more strongly to upsetting images, and its connection with the calming medial PFC was weaker. Less sleep, less top-down control.
Narcolepsy and cataplexyLoss of the neurons in the lateral hypothalamus that make orexin (also called hypocretin), which normally stabilises wakefulness. The result is overwhelming daytime sleepiness, falling straight into REM, and cataplexy: REM's muscle atonia suddenly breaking into wakefulness, usually triggered by strong emotion. The person stays conscious but goes limp.
REM sleep behaviour disorderThe opposite failure: the brainstem circuit that paralyses the body during REM doesn't work, so people physically act out their dreams by punching, kicking or shouting. It's most common in older men and can be an early warning sign of Parkinson's disease.

  • Declarative: episodic (events) and semantic (facts); hippocampus. Nondeclarative: procedural, priming, conditioning.
  • H.M.: bilateral MTL removed. Anterograde amnesia, some graded retrograde, skills spared.
  • Anterograde = no new memories. Retrograde = lose old ones; recent lost first.
  • Encoding (attention) → consolidation (fragile) → storage → retrieval. Encoding failure: never stored. Retrieval failure: a cue helps.
  • Reconstruction; misinformation (Loftus). Amygdala boosts emotional consolidation; flashbulb = vivid, not necessarily accurate.
  • Eyeblink: cerebellum. Fear conditioning: amygdala.
  • LTP: glutamate; AMPA; NMDA = coincidence detector (glutamate + depolarization pops Mg²⁺) → Ca²⁺ → more AMPA, bigger spines, more release.

4 · Emotion

  • James-Lange: body → feeling. Cannon-Bard: thalamus → both at once. Schachter-Singer: arousal + label (misattribution). Facial feedback: expression shapes feeling.
  • LeDoux: low road thalamus→amygdala (fast, crude); high road via cortex (slow, accurate).
  • Klüver-Bucy: amygdala/temporal damage gives tameness, no fear, hyperorality.
  • Cortex restrains hypothalamic aggression (sham rage without it).
  • Psychopathy: hypoactive amygdala, low fear and empathy cues.
  • VTA → nucleus accumbens dopamine. RPE: surprise = burst; predicted = none at reward (burst moves to cue); omitted = dip.

5 · Stress

  • SAM: sympathetic → adrenal medulla → epi/NE. Seconds.
  • HPA: hypothalamus CRH → anterior pituitary ACTH → adrenal cortex cortisol. Minutes–hours.
  • Cortisol: raises glucose; suppresses immune, digestion, growth, reproduction; negative feedback.
  • Acute (zebra) = adaptive. Chronic (rumination) = immune, heart, hippocampal harm.

6 · Rhythms

  • Circadian ≈ 24 h; ultradian < 24 h (90-min sleep cycle); infradian > 24 h (monthly; circannual = yearly).
  • SCN = master clock; free-runs ~24.2–25 h; zeitgebers (light most).
  • Blue light → melanopsin ipRGCs → retinohypothalamic tract → SCN → pineal → melatonin (at night). ipRGC loss: entrainment, not sight.
  • Jet lag: east is harder (advance). Morning light advances, evening light delays. Teens: phase delay.

6 · Sleep

  • Awake beta/alpha → N1 theta → N2 spindles + K-complexes → N3 delta (deepest) → REM (desynchronized, eyes, dreams, atonia).
  • SWS early, REM late; cutting sleep short costs REM.
  • Functions: glymphatic clearance (beta-amyloid), consolidation + hippocampal replay, development (infants, lots of REM), emotion (Yoo 2007: amygdala up, mPFC link down).
  • Narcolepsy: orexin/hypocretin loss; cataplexy. RBD: atonia fails, dreams acted out.
