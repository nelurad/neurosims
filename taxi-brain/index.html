The Taxi Driver's Brain

To drive a black cab in London, you have to memorize the whole city. No sat-nav allowed. Scientists scanned these drivers' brains and found that all that learning had changed the shape of one small, seahorse-shaped part: the hippocampus. Hop in and we'll see how.

25,000 streetswithin six miles of Charing Cross, plus thousands of landmarks. Learning them is called "the Knowledge."
3 to 4 yearsof study, usually on a moped, before the exams. About half the trainees never pass.
Bigger at the backDrivers have more gray matter in the back of the hippocampus, and more of it the longer they have been driving.

    Stop 2

    Meet the seahorse

    In plain wordsDeep inside each temporal lobe sits a curled-up structure called the hippocampus (Greek for "seahorse"). You have two, one on each side. Its front end sits near your ears, close to the amygdala. Its back end curls up toward the middle of your head.

    Drive for years. Watch it change.

    Years driving0

    In Maguire and colleagues' (2000) study, the more years someone had driven a cab, the bigger the back of their right hippocampus was. The front was a little smaller. The real differences are only a few percent, so the drawing makes them about five times bigger so you can see them.

    Stop 3

    How a new street gets stored

    In plain wordsMeet Priya, a trainee. Today she rides her moped down Hatton Garden, a little street full of jewelry shops. Follow that memory step by step: in through the doorway, around the hippocampus, and back out to the cortex, where it will live for years.
    How strong the memory isweak

    Strengthening synapses with repeated use is called long-term potentiation (LTP); see The Learning Brain. Dentate gyrus "pattern separation" and CA3 "pattern completion" are the leading ideas for what these areas do, but they are still models, not settled facts. Moving memories out to the cortex over time is called consolidation; see Kinds of Memory.

    Stop 4

    What the scans showed

    In plain wordsEleanor Maguire's team put 16 London cabbies in an MRI scanner and compared them with 50 men who didn't drive taxis. The cabbies had more gray matter at the back of the hippocampus and less at the front. The longer they had been driving, the bigger the back part was.

    But wait. Maybe it's just driving?

    Driving all day is stressful, and you sit in traffic. So in 2006 the team compared cabbies with London bus drivers. Bus drivers drive just as much, but on the same fixed routes. They don't need a mental map.

    Only the taxi drivers had the bigger back hippocampus, and only in taxi drivers did it grow with years on the job. So it's the map, not the driving.

    Each dot is a made-up driver, generated to match the pattern in Maguire et al. (2000): drivers had 1.5 to 42 years on the job, and right posterior hippocampus volume rose with years (r ≈ .6). The scans measured gray matter with voxel-based morphometry, which compares brain tissue point by point between groups.

    Stop 5

    Chicken or egg?

    In plain wordsMaybe people with a big hippocampus are just good at directions, so they become cabbies. To find out, scientists had to scan trainees before they learned the Knowledge, then again years later.

    Nothing comes for free

    Everyone also copied a tricky made-up drawing, then drew it from memory 30 minutes later. At scan 2, the new cabbies had become worse at this than the others.

    Bars show the direction of the result, not the exact scores.

    Woollett and Maguire (2011) followed 79 trainees and 31 non-drivers. On day one, the groups had the same hippocampus and the same memory scores. The 39 trainees who passed showed more gray matter in the posterior hippocampus 3 to 4 years later; the 20 who didn't pass and the controls showed no change. One idea for the memory cost is that a hippocampus packed with one huge map has less room for new visual information, but nobody knows for sure yet.

    Stop 6

    Graph paper inside your head

    In plain wordsLondon has no grid, but your brain brings its own. Just next to the hippocampus, in the entorhinal cortex, grid cells fire at spots arranged in triangles, like a sheet of graph paper laid over the world. Next door, the hippocampus's place cells fire at one spot each. Together they work like a GPS.

    Move your mouse (or tap) to drive the cab around.

    Grid cells come in sizes: small grids, medium grids, large grids. One size alone can't tell you where you are, because its pattern repeats everywhere. Switch on more sizes and see where they all agree.

    Grid cells were found in rats by May-Britt and Edvard Moser's lab (Hafting et al., 2005), and place cells by John O'Keefe (1971); they shared the 2014 Nobel Prize. Brain scans show grid-like signals in the human entorhinal cortex during virtual navigation too (Doeller et al., 2010). Real grid sizes step up by about 1.4 times from one module to the next (Stensola et al., 2012), which is what this drawing uses. Meet place cells in a running rat in The Remembering Brain.

    Stop 7

    Zoomed out at the front, zoomed in at the back

    In plain wordsThe hippocampus isn't the same from end to end. Place cells near the back fire in small spots, so they know exactly which corner you're on. Place cells near the front fire across big areas, so they know the neighborhood. A cabbie needs a lot of street-level detail, and that's the part that grew.

    Slide along the seahorse

    ← Front (anterior)Back (posterior) →

    In rats, place fields grow from under a meter at one end of the hippocampus to around 10 meters at the other (Kjelstrup et al., 2008); the rat's "dorsal" end matches our posterior end. The idea that the human anterior hippocampus handles the big picture and the posterior handles fine detail is popular and fits a lot of data (Poppenk et al., 2013), but it is still being tested. The front end also connects strongly to the amygdala and hypothalamus, so it is more involved in emotion and stress.

    Stop 8

    Take a ride in the scanner

    In plain wordsTaxi drivers lay in an MRI scanner and drove around a very realistic video game version of London. Afterward, they described what they had been thinking at each moment. Different parts of the trip lit up different parts of the brain. Click each stop on the ride.

    Based on Spiers and Maguire (2006), who scanned cabbies driving through a video game London; Javadi et al. (2017), who found that the posterior hippocampus tracks how many streets branch off as you enter a new one, the prefrontal cortex responds at forced detours, and both go quiet when you just follow directions; and Hartley et al. (2003), who found that following a well-learned route uses the caudate, while finding a new way uses the hippocampus. Areas on the inner surface of the brain are drawn as if seen through it.

    Last stop

    Check yourself

    In plain wordsThe big lesson: the adult brain is not fixed. Years of practice at one thing can change its structure, even if the change is small, and it may come with a cost.
