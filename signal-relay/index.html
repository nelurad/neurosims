Signal Relay

Something touches your hand. You hear a song. You smell coffee. Each of those signals has to travel from a sense organ to the right part of the brain, and almost all of them make the same stop on the way: the thalamus, the brain’s relay station. Almost all. Your job is to route each signal, and catch the exceptions.

1Route the signal

Read what just happened, then tap the brain areas in the order the signal reaches them. Get it right on the first try for full points. Watch out: one sense skips the thalamus entirely, and some signals trigger a reflex before your brain even knows.

2Cheat sheet: every sense’s route

The pattern to remember: sense organ → thalamus → primary cortex. Each sense has its own relay nucleus in the thalamus. Smell is the one that breaks the rule.

🏢 Why a relay station?

The thalamus isn’t just a hallway. It filters and boosts signals depending on what you’re paying attention to, and it gates sensory input during sleep. That’s one reason you can sleep through a quiet room but wake to your name.

👃 Why does smell skip it?

Smell is the oldest sense in evolution. Its receptors send axons straight to the olfactory bulb, which feeds olfactory cortex right next to the amygdala and hippocampus. That direct line to emotion and memory is why a smell can bring back a memory instantly. (Smell does reach the thalamus later, on its way to the orbitofrontal cortex, but not first.)

🔥 Why reflexes come first

When you touch something hot, pain fibers connect to interneurons in the spinal cord, which fire the motor neurons that pull your hand away. That loop never waits for the brain, so you move before you feel it. The pain signal keeps climbing to the thalamus and cortex, which is when you think “ouch.”

↔️ Everything crosses

The thalamus and cortex on the left side of your brain handle the right side of your body, and vice versa. Smell is the exception here too: each nostril mostly reports to the same side.
