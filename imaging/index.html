Seeing the Brain

The brain is locked inside a skull, made of billions of cells too small to see, and its signals last thousandths of a second. No single tool captures all of that. This tour shows the main ways neuroscientists look at brain structure, watch it work, switch parts of it on and off, and stain its cells, plus what each method is good for and where it falls short.

    Part 1

    Every method trades space for time

    Two questions decide which tool to use. Where: how small a piece of brain can it tell apart (spatial resolution)? When: how quickly can it follow changes (temporal resolution)? A third question is how invasive it is. Click a box to read about each method.

    Pick a method

    Each box spans the range of sizes (up and down) and time scales (left and right) a method can resolve. Blue boxes are noninvasive and can be used in healthy people; red ones need surgery, injections into the brain, or brain tissue.

    The ideal method would sit in the bottom-left corner: single cells, millisecond by millisecond, in a whole living human brain, without opening the skull. Nothing does. That is why researchers combine methods.

    Values are approximate ranges for typical use in research; boxes for structural scans, lesions and histology sit at the right because they give a snapshot of structure, not a moment-to-moment signal.

    Part 2 · Structure

    Computerized tomography (CT)

    A CT scanner spins an X-ray source around the head and measures how much of each beam gets through. A computer then reconstructs a slice ("tomo" = slice). Dense tissue like bone blocks X-rays and looks white; fluid looks dark. Fresh blood is denser than brain, so a bleed shows up bright. Pick a patient.

    CT · axial slicehealthy
    Rotate the X-ray tubeall angles

    A healthy brain

    Drag the rotate slider down: with only a few projection angles the computer cannot work out where each density came from, so the slice turns into streaks. Collecting views from all around the head is what lets it reconstruct a clean slice.

    Good for

    • Emergencies: strokes, bleeds, skull fractures, tumors
    • Fast (seconds) and widely available
    • Works with metal implants

    Limits

    • Uses ionizing radiation (X-rays)
    • Poor contrast between gray and white matter
    • Structure only, no activity

    By convention, medical scans are shown as if you were looking up from the patient's feet, so the patient's left appears on the right of the image. In the emergency room, CT is the first scan for a suspected stroke because it quickly rules out bleeding before clot-busting drugs are given.

    Part 3 · Structure

    Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

    Your body is full of water, and every water molecule has hydrogen nuclei (protons) that act like tiny magnets. A powerful magnet lines them up, a radio pulse knocks them over, and as they relax back they give off radio signals. Gray matter, white matter and fluid relax at different rates, which gives MRI its detail. Move through the brain in any of the three planes.

    MRI · sagittalT1-weighted
    Left ↔ rightmidline
    Protons point every which way. Turn on the magnet.

    Good for

    • Fine anatomical detail (about 1 mm, finer in research scanners)
    • Clear gray vs. white matter contrast
    • No ionizing radiation

    Limits

    • Expensive, loud, and you must lie still in a narrow tube
    • No metal: pacemakers and some implants are unsafe
    • Plain MRI shows structure, not activity

    In this T1-weighted image white matter (fatty myelin) looks bright, gray matter is darker gray, and fluid in the ventricles is black. Other settings (T2) flip the contrast so fluid looks bright. The slices here are drawn by a simple model for teaching, not taken from a real person.

    Part 4 · Wiring

    Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)

    Water inside a bundle of axons can only flow along the bundle, like water in a pack of straws. DTI is an MRI scan that measures which way water flows at each spot, and a computer connects the dots into the brain's wiring. Colors show which way each fiber runs.

    Reading the colors

    left ↔ right
    front ↔ back
    up ↔ down
    This is the brain seen from above, front at the top. Tap a button to pick out one kind of fiber.

    Good for

    • Seeing the brain's wiring in living people
    • Finding white matter damage (injury, MS)

    Limits

    • Guesses fibers from water flow; doesn't see axons
    • Gets confused where fibers cross

    Part 5 · Function

    Functional MRI (fMRI)

    Active neurons use energy, and within a few seconds the blood vessels around them deliver extra oxygen-rich blood, more than the neurons use. Oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor hemoglobin affect the MRI signal differently, so the scanner picks up this BOLD (blood-oxygen-level-dependent) signal. Choose a task and run the scan.

    BOLD signal in the active region
    The shaded bar is when the task is on. Watch how late the blood flow response peaks.

    Finding the signal: the subtraction method

    The brain is never quiet. Even at rest, nearly every region is active. To find what a task adds, researchers scan the same person during the task and during a control condition that matches everything except the thing of interest, then subtract. Averaging many trials removes the random noise.

    Trials averaged1 trial
    With one trial the difference image is mostly noise. Average more trials.

    Good for

    • Locating activity to a few millimeters, across the whole brain, including deep structures
    • No radiation or injections, so people can be scanned many times

    Limits

    • Slow: blood flow lags neural activity by seconds
    • Indirect: measures blood, not neurons
    • Shows correlation; cannot prove a region is needed
    • Same MRI limits: cost, noise, no metal, must lie still

    Part 6 · Function

    Positron emission tomography (PET)

    A short-lived radioactive tracer is injected into the bloodstream. The most common is FDG, a form of glucose that active brain cells take up and trap. As the tracer decays it gives off positrons, which produce pairs of gamma rays that the scanner detects. The result is a color-coded map of where the tracer collected.

    FDG-PET · axialhealthy
    low high glucose use

    Healthy

    PET is not limited to glucose. Other tracers bind dopamine receptors or transporters (used in Parkinson's disease research), or the amyloid and tau proteins that build up in Alzheimer's disease.

    Good for

    • Measuring specific chemistry: glucose, receptors, proteins
    • Diagnosing and tracking diseases such as Alzheimer's

    Limits

    • Radioactive injection limits repeat scans
    • Slow (tens of seconds to minutes) and blurry (several mm)
    • Expensive; needs a cyclotron nearby for short-lived tracers

    Part 7 · Function

    Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS)

    Near-infrared light (about 700–1000 nm) passes through skin and bone surprisingly well. A source on the scalp shines it in; it scatters around in the tissue, and a detector a few centimeters away catches the light that makes it back out. Oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor hemoglobin absorb these wavelengths differently, so changes in the light reveal changes in blood oxygen in the cortex beneath.

    Source–detector distance3.0 cm

    Good for

    • Portable, quiet and cheap; can be worn while moving
    • Infants, children, and people who cannot use MRI
    • Natural settings: conversation, walking, classrooms

    Limits

    • Only reaches the outer cortex (about 1–2 cm deep)
    • Coarse spatial resolution (around 1–3 cm)
    • Slow, like fMRI, because it also tracks blood

    Part 8 · Function

    EEG and MEG: millisecond timing

    When thousands of neighboring neurons are active together, their currents add up. EEG picks up the resulting voltages with electrodes on the scalp (see The Sleeping Brain for how). Every electric current also makes a magnetic field, and MEG measures those tiny fields with ultrasensitive sensors in a helmet. Show the participant a face or an object.

    Magnetic field over right occipitotemporal sensors
    Faces evoke a larger response about 170 ms after they appear, generated in the fusiform gyrus on the underside of the temporal lobe, usually stronger on the right.

    Good for

    • Millisecond timing of brain responses
    • MEG fields pass through the skull undistorted, so sources are easier to locate than with EEG
    • EEG is cheap and portable

    Limits

    • Hard to pin down where deep signals come from
    • MEG needs a magnetically shielded room and costly sensors (traditionally cooled with liquid helium)
    • EEG signals are smeared by the skull and scalp

    Part 9 · Stimulation

    Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)

    Imaging shows which areas are active, but not whether they are needed. TMS can test that. A figure-8 coil held against the head sends a brief, strong magnetic pulse that passes through the skull and induces an electric current in the cortex beneath, making neurons fire or briefly disrupting what they were doing. Drag the coil, then fire a pulse.

    Drag the coil over the head. Try the hand area of motor cortex, the back of the head, and low on the left frontal lobe.

    Coil over: scalp

    Fire a pulse to see what happens.

    Good for

    • Testing whether a region is necessary (a temporary "virtual lesion") in healthy people
    • Mapping motor cortex before surgery
    • Repeated TMS (rTMS) is FDA-cleared to treat depression
    • Can be combined with EEG or imaging

    Limits

    • Reaches only the outer cortex, a few cm deep
    • Spreads over roughly a centimeter or more
    • Loud click, scalp twitches; a small risk of seizure

    The figure-8 shape concentrates the induced current under the point where the two loops meet. TMS was introduced by Barker and colleagues in 1985, who showed that a pulse over motor cortex makes a hand muscle twitch.

    Part 10 · Cells

    Histology: staining the cells themselves

    To see individual neurons, researchers usually need brain tissue: from animals, from biopsies, or donated after death. The tissue is cut into slices thinner than a hair and treated with stains that label different things. Switch stains on the same piece of tissue.

    Nissl50 µm ▬

    These micrographs are generated by the page to show what each stain reveals; they are not photographs. The Golgi stain's habit of labeling only a few percent of neurons, completely, is still not fully understood. Cajal used it to argue that neurons are separate cells, which won him and Golgi a shared Nobel Prize in 1906.
