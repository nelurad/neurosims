    Part 1

    A map of memory systems

    In plain wordsThere isn't one "memory." There are many kinds. Facts and life events are one kind. Skills, habits and learned fears are others, and each kind relies on its own brain areas.

    Long-term memory splits into declarative memory, which you can consciously state ("knowing that"), and nondeclarative memory, shown by performance ("knowing how"). Click the boxes to open each branch, then click any memory type to light up the brain regions it depends on.

    Every type of memory depends on a different mix of brain regions. Many regions take part in memory, and no single "memory center" stores everything.

    Part 2

    Capture, hold, store, retrieve

    In plain wordsTo remember anything, it has to get in, stay and come back out. Most of what hits your eyes and ears never makes it past step one, simply because you weren't paying attention to it.

    Information flows through stages. Sensory buffers hold a fleeting copy of everything for a fraction of a second to a few seconds. What you attend to is encoded into short-term memory, which lasts about 30 seconds unless you rehearse. Some of it is consolidated into long-term memory, and later retrieved back into use. Information can be lost at every step.

    Attentionsome

    How much is in the sensory buffer?

    Sperling (1960) flashed 12 letters. People could report only about 4 of them, but when a tone right after the flash told them which row to report, they got most of that row, so most letters were briefly available. Try it.

    Short-term memory fades without rehearsal

    In the Petersons' (1959) task, people saw three letters, then counted backward by threes so they could not rehearse. Recall fell steeply within seconds. Let them rehearse and the letters stay.

    Capacity is limited too: Miller (1956) suggested about 7 ± 2 items; later work suggests about 4 chunks (Cowan, 2001). Working memory is the active use of what is in short-term memory, and it relies heavily on the prefrontal cortex.

    Peterson curve values are approximate. Figures for buffers: visual (iconic) memory lasts well under a second; auditory (echoic) memory lasts a few seconds.

    Part 3

    Consolidation: from hippocampus to cortex

    In plain wordsNew memories are fragile at first and lean on the hippocampus. Over time, and especially while you sleep, they get filed into the rest of the brain and become sturdy. That's why old memories survive brain injuries better than new ones.

    A new declarative memory is a pattern spread across many cortical areas (what you saw, heard, felt), tied together by the hippocampus. Over days to years, the cortical pieces link up with each other directly and the memory depends less on the hippocampus. Much of this happens during slow-wave sleep, when the hippocampus replays the day.

    Why retrograde amnesia hits recent memories hardest: they were still hippocampus-dependent. Concussions, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and hippocampal damage erase what had not finished consolidating.

    This is the standard model of systems consolidation (e.g., Squire & Alvarez, 1995). Some researchers argue that vivid episodic detail always needs the hippocampus. Sleep link: slow-wave sleep after learning especially helps declarative memories; see The Sleeping Brain.

    Part 4

    Where is a memory stored?

    In plain wordsA memory isn't kept in one spot. The sights, sounds and smells are stored in the brain areas that handle each sense, and the hippocampus links them. That's why one cue, like a smell, can bring the whole scene flooding back.

    There is no single file for "grandma's kitchen". The pieces live in the cortical areas that first processed them: sights in visual cortex, sounds in auditory cortex, smells in olfactory cortex, feelings via the amygdala. Recalling the memory reinstates that pattern. A single cue, like the smell of cookies, can bring back the rest (pattern completion). Try it.

    The memory trace (sometimes called an engram) is distributed. The hippocampus acts like an index that points to all the pieces.
    Huge capacity. People shown 10,000 pictures for a few seconds each recognized most of them days later (Standing, 1973). In another study people remembered which of two nearly identical objects they had seen among 2,500, about 87% of the time (Brady et al., 2008). No one has found the upper limit of long-term memory.
    How do the synapses store it? Changes in synaptic strength, such as long-term potentiation (LTP) through NMDA and AMPA receptors, are the leading candidate. That story, plus enriched environments and the sea slug Aplysia, is on the companion page: The Learning Brain.
