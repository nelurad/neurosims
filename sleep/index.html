The Sleeping Brain

You spend about a third of your life asleep, but your brain is far from off. Every night it cycles through distinct states, each with its own brain waves, chemistry and jobs.

    Brain waves: what an EEG sees

    Sleep is defined by three signals recorded at once: EEG (brain waves from the scalp), EOG (eye movements) and EMG (chin muscle tone). Pick a state and watch how all three change. As you fall deeper asleep, the brain waves get slower and bigger, because more neurons fire together in sync.

    Part 3

    Why you get sleepy: two forces

    Sleep pressure (Process S) builds every hour you are awake as adenosine accumulates, and drains while you sleep. Your body clock (Process C, run by the SCN) pushes alertness up and down over 24 hours. You fall asleep easily when pressure rises above the clock's alerting signal. Try an all-nighter, a coffee, or a nap, and drag across the two days.

    Part 4

    Sleep in the brain: the flip-flop switch

    Wake-promoting centers in the brainstem and hypothalamus and the sleep-promoting VLPO inhibit each other, like a seesaw that is either up or down, so you switch between states quickly instead of drifting. Orexin holds the switch steady. A separate switch in the pons turns on REM and paralyzes your muscles. Pick a state, then click any region.

    Part 5

    What is sleep for?

    Every animal studied sleeps, even though sleeping leaves it defenseless. So sleep must do something that cannot be done while awake. Scientists have found several jobs, and they likely all matter.

    Waste in the brain
    The glymphatic system

    Brain cells make waste as they work, including beta-amyloid, the protein that clumps in Alzheimer's disease. In sleeping mice, the space between brain cells grew by about 60%, and cerebrospinal fluid flushed beta-amyloid out about twice as fast as during waking (Xie et al., 2013).

    In people, large pulses of fluid wash through the brain during NREM sleep, timed to the slow waves (Fultz et al., 2019). Still debated: a 2024 mouse study measured less clearance during sleep (Miao et al., 2024), so expect this story to keep developing.
    Replaying the day

    Hippocampal place cells fire in order as a rat runs a track. During NREM sleep the same sequence replays, compressed about 10–20× faster, during bursts called sharp-wave ripples (Wilson & McNaughton, 1994).

    Ripples tend to arrive with sleep spindles and cortical slow waves, which is thought to move memories from the hippocampus into long-term storage in the cortex. Sleep after learning improves recall more than the same time spent awake (Diekelmann & Born, 2010).
    The synaptic homeostasis idea

    Learning while awake strengthens synapses. That costs energy and space, and if it never stopped, everything would end up strong and nothing would stand out. The idea is that sleep scales all synapses back down, keeping their relative strengths, so important connections survive and weak, noisy ones fade (Tononi & Cirelli, 2014).

    Evidence: in mouse cortex, the contacts between neurons were about 18% smaller after sleep than after waking (de Vivo et al., 2017).

    Growth & repair

    The biggest pulse of growth hormone each day comes during the first deep N3 period of the night.

    Immune defense

    When volunteers were exposed to a cold virus, people who slept under 6 hours were about 4× more likely to get sick than those sleeping over 7 (Prather et al., 2015).

    Emotional reset

    REM sleep is when the brain replays emotional memories with stress chemicals such as norepinephrine switched off. It may take the sting out of hard experiences ("overnight therapy"; Walker & van der Helm, 2009).

    Saving energy

    Metabolism and body temperature fall during NREM sleep. The saving is modest (~10%), so energy alone can't explain why we sleep.

    Appetite & metabolism

    After two nights of only 4 hours, the fullness hormone leptin dropped 18%, the hunger hormone ghrelin rose 28%, and cravings for high-carb foods went up (Spiegel et al., 2004).

    It's essential

    Rats kept completely awake died within about 2–3 weeks (Rechtschaffen & Bergmann). In people, the rare genetic disease fatal familial insomnia destroys the thalamus and with it the ability to sleep.

      The sleep debt you don't feel

      Volunteers were allowed 8, 6 or 4 hours in bed every night for two weeks (Van Dongen et al., 2003). Attention lapses in the 6-hour group kept climbing until they matched people who had gone one to two full nights without any sleep.

      But switch to how sleepy they felt: their ratings leveled off after a few days. People who are chronically short on sleep underestimate how impaired they are.
