    What is a memory, anyway?

    In plain wordsA memory is a change in your brain that lets the past shape what you do now. Remembering your friend's name, knowing how to ride a bike and flinching at the sound of a dentist's drill are all memories. Your brain keeps each kind in a different way, in different places.
    1

    Get it in

    Encoding. You can only remember what you paid attention to. Your senses take in a flood of information, and attention picks what gets through.

    Can't find your keys? You probably weren't paying attention when you put them down.
    2

    Keep it

    Storage. New memories are fragile. The hippocampus holds them together while they slowly settle into the rest of the brain. This is called consolidation, and sleep gives it a big boost.

    That's why an all-nighter before an exam backfires.
    3

    Get it back

    Retrieval. A memory only helps if you can find it. Cues like a smell, a place or a question help you pull it out, and every time you do, your brain rebuilds it a little.

    A word on the tip of your tongue is stored. You just can't reach it yet.

    A sticky note and a library

    Short-term memory is like a sticky note: it holds just a few things, about 4 chunks, for around 30 seconds unless you keep repeating them. Long-term memory is like a huge library that can hold a lifetime of facts, faces and skills.

    Do Round 1, then Round 2, and compare your scores.

    Words you'll see a lot

    Short-term memoryHolds a few things for seconds, like a phone number you're about to dial.
    Long-term memoryStores things for days to a lifetime. Huge capacity.
    HippocampusThe sea-horse-shaped structure that saves new facts and events.
    Declarative memory"Knowing that": facts and events you can put into words.
    Nondeclarative memory"Knowing how": skills, habits and reflexes you show by doing.
    ConsolidationThe slow process that turns a fragile new memory into a sturdy one.
    AmnesiaMemory loss. Anterograde = can't make new memories. Retrograde = lost old ones.
    AmygdalaThe brain's alarm system. It makes emotional moments extra memorable.

    Part 1

    Patient H.M.: the present kept vanishing

    In plain wordsOne man's brain surgery taught us where new memories are made. After a deep part of both sides of his brain was removed, Henry could still talk, joke, think and remember his childhood. But anything new slipped away within minutes, like writing on a whiteboard that kept getting erased.

    In 1953, a 27-year-old named Henry Molaison had surgery for epilepsy so severe that drugs could not control it. The surgeon removed the medial temporal lobe on both sides: much of the hippocampus, the amygdala and the nearby cortex. The seizures improved, but Henry could no longer form new long-term memories of facts and events. He met his doctors again and again as strangers for the rest of his life.

    Two kinds of amnesia

    Retrograde amnesia erases memories from before the injury (backward in time). Anterograde amnesia blocks new memories after it (forward). Pick a case and see which memories survive.

    LostForming new long-term memories of facts and events. Some memories from the years just before surgery.
    KeptIQ, personality, language, short-term memory (he could hold a number in mind by rehearsing it), most older memories, and the ability to learn new skills.
    Similar surgeries that spared the hippocampus did not cause this amnesia. So the hippocampus, with the cortex around it, is the critical structure for turning new experiences into lasting memories.
    Memory is not one thing. H.M. showed that short-term memory, long-term storage of old memories, and skill learning can all work while one specific ability, making new declarative memories, is gone.

    The timelines are illustrative. Retrograde amnesia is usually graded: recent memories are lost more than old ones, because recent memories had not finished consolidating (Ribot's law). H.M. lost some memories from roughly the decade before surgery.

    Part 2

    Where new memories are made

    In plain wordsThe hippocampus is a curled structure tucked deep inside each side of your brain, shaped a bit like a sea horse. Think of it as the brain's "save" button for new facts and events.

    The hippocampus (Latin for "sea horse") curls along the inner floor of each temporal lobe. Its output bundle, the fornix, arches up and forward to the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus. In the drawing the temporal lobe is see-through so you can find it.

    Compare the curl of the hippocampus with a sea horse's body and tail. The head of the hippocampus sits toward the front of the temporal lobe, near the amygdala; the tail curves up at the back and continues as the fornix.

    A slice through the temporal lobes

    Cut the brain vertically from ear to ear (a coronal slice) and the hippocampus appears as a small curled structure on each side, just under the fluid-filled horn of the lateral ventricle. Click the regions in the close-up, then switch on H.M.'s lesion.

    Simulated MRI (T1-style: white matter bright, gray matter darker, fluid black).

    Click a region.

    Entorhinal and perirhinal cortex lie toward the front of the medial temporal lobe and parahippocampal cortex further back; the close-up combines them in one view. Brain scans of H.M. (Corkin et al., 1997; Annese et al., 2014) showed that the front of the hippocampus, the amygdala and entorhinal cortex were removed, while a shrunken back part of the hippocampus and most parahippocampal cortex remained. His cerebellum, which the surgeons never touched, also shrank over the decades, most likely from long-term use of the antiseizure drug phenytoin.

    Part 3

    Where am I? Place cells and grid cells

    In plain wordsYour brain has a built-in map. Some neurons fire only when you're in one particular spot in a room. Others mark out a pattern like graph paper, so your brain always knows where you are.

    Record from single neurons in a rat's hippocampus as it explores, and each place cell fires only when the rat is in one particular spot, its place field (O'Keefe & Dostrovsky, 1971). Together they form a cognitive map. Next door in the entorhinal cortex, grid cells fire at many spots that form a hexagonal grid, like map coordinates (Hafting et al., 2005). Watch the fields appear, or steer the rat with your pointer.

    Nobel Prize 2014: John O'Keefe, May-Britt Moser and Edvard Moser, for discovering the brain's positioning system. Place cells and grid cells have since been recorded in people too.
    Bigger maps for bigger jobs. London taxi drivers, who memorize thousands of streets, have a larger posterior hippocampus (Maguire et al., 2000), and birds that hide food in many places have larger hippocampi than related birds that don't.

    Part 4

    Learning without remembering

    In plain wordsHenry got better at a tricky drawing task every single day, yet never remembered doing it before. So knowing how (skills) and knowing that (facts) must use different brain systems.

    Brenda Milner gave H.M. a hard task: trace a star while seeing your hand only in a mirror. Over three days his errors dropped just like a healthy person's, yet each day he said he had never done the task before. Try it: the mirror flips up and down. Press inside the star near the dot and trace the path between the lines.

    Errors: 0 · Progress: 0%
    Each completed star adds a point. H.M.'s curve shows the pattern Milner reported: errors fall within each day, and the improvement carries over to the next day.
    Knowing that vs. knowing how. Facts and events (declarative memory) need the hippocampus. Skills (procedural memory) use other systems, mainly the basal ganglia, motor cortex and cerebellum, so they survived H.M.'s surgery. He also learned to read mirror-reversed words.

    H.M.'s curve is an illustrative redrawing of the shape of Milner's (1962) data (ten trials a day for three days), not exact values.

    Part 5

    More patients, one memory circuit

    In plain wordsIt's not just the hippocampus. Damage to a few connected brain parts causes the same kind of memory loss, because they work as a team, like stops along one train line. Break any stop and the train can't get through.

    Different injuries in different places can cause the same amnesia, which points to a connected circuit: medial temporal lobe, fornix, mammillary bodies and the dorsomedial thalamus. Some patients also show that episodic and semantic memory can come apart.

    Diencephalon

    Patient N.A.

    A miniature fencing foil went up his nostril into the brain, damaging the dorsomedial thalamus and the pathway to the mammillary bodies. Like H.M.: short-term memory fine, but few new declarative memories.

    Diencephalon

    Korsakoff's syndrome

    Caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, usually from chronic alcoholism. Damages the mammillary bodies, dorsomedial thalamus and frontal cortex. Patients confabulate: they fill memory gaps with made-up stories they believe.

    Episodic vs. semantic

    Patient K.C.

    After a motorcycle accident, K.C. still knew facts about the world and about himself (semantic memory) but could not relive a single specific event from his own life (episodic memory).

    Monkeys show the same deficit

    Delayed non-matching-to-sample: a monkey sees an object, waits, then must pick the new object to get a treat. Monkeys with lesions like H.M.'s do fine after a few seconds but fail at longer delays.

    Values approximate the pattern reported by Zola-Morgan, Squire and colleagues: normal monkeys stay near 90% correct; lesioned monkeys drop toward chance (50%) as the delay grows.
