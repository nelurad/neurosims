Sensation in the Brain

Every part of your body has its own patch of brain. Two strips of cortex run over the top of your head like a headband, one on each side of a deep groove called the central sulcus. The strip in front controls movement. The strip behind it feels touch. Your body is laid out along each strip in order, but the space isn’t shared fairly: your hands, lips and tongue get far more brain than your back.

3Follow the signal

Why does touching your hand light up the hand patch? Because the nerve fibers from your hand are wired to that exact spot. Pick a body part and send a signal. Notice that the left brain feels and moves the right side of the body.

    The brain in this picture is a simplified front-on slice. In a real brain the touch strip and movement strip sit side by side (front to back), so one flat slice can’t show both. The picture shows whichever strip you picked. The spinal cord is drawn much shorter than it really is.

    4So is the homunculus real?

    Yes, in the sense that matters: each body part really does have its own patch of cortex, laid out in order, and the patches really are different sizes. But the map is alive and a little messy.

    Penfield’s electrode

    In the 1930s to 1950s, Wilder Penfield mapped these strips in awake patients during epilepsy surgery. The brain has no pain receptors, so it doesn’t hurt. A tiny current on the back strip made patients feel tingling in one body part. On the front strip, a body part twitched. He drew the results as the “homunculus” (Latin for “little man”).

    Practice grows the map

    Violinists press the strings with their left-hand fingers all day. Brain scans show the left-hand finger area in their touch strip is larger than in non-musicians, especially if they started young. Reading Braille enlarges the reading finger’s area too.

    Phantom touch

    After someone loses a hand, the hand area doesn’t go quiet. Its neighbor on the strip, the face area, can spread into it. Touching the cheek can then feel like touching the missing hand.

    The borders are fuzzy

    Neighboring body parts overlap on the strip, finger areas share territory, and every person’s map is a bit different. A 2023 study found that the movement strip also has patches between the foot, hand and mouth areas that help plan whole-body actions. These patches break up the classic map.

    The sizes here are approximate. The share of the strip comes from Penfield’s maps. The share of skin uses the “rule of nines” that doctors use to estimate burn area, and two-point thresholds come from Weinstein (1968). Inside the mouth and the gut have almost no skin, so they get no skin bar. Penfield placed the genitals below the foot on the inner wall, but some newer brain scans put them nearer the hip.
