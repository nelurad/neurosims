Exam 1

The nervous system's layout, the brain's parts, and how we study them

1

Afferent, efferent, and who controls breathing

Signals going toward the brain and spinal cord are afferent. Signals going away from them are efferent. A few behaviors, like breathing, are run by two control systems at once.

The mix-up

Afferent neurons carry movement commands out; efferent neurons bring sensations in.

Breathing is controlled only by the autonomic nervous system, and paying attention temporarily overrides it.

Actually

Afferent arrives (sensory in). Efferent exits (motor out).

The diaphragm is skeletal muscle. The brainstem drives it automatically, and the motor cortex can take over on purpose. Both send their commands through the same motor neurons in the spinal cord.

Arrives vs. exits

Touch the stove and follow the signal in, then back out.

Two drivers, one set of motor neurons

Pick a situation and see which path is in charge of the diaphragm.

What are phrenic motor neurons? They are the motor neurons that make the diaphragm contract. Their cell bodies sit in the ventral horn of the spinal cord at neck levels C3–C5. Their axons travel together down to the diaphragm as the phrenic nerve, one on each side. Like any motor neuron that controls skeletal muscle, they are somatic efferents. They are also the last stop in the chain, so every breathing command passes through them, whether it comes from the brainstem or the cortex. (Memory hook: "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive.")

Remember it: SAME = Sensory Afferent, Motor Efferent. "Autonomic" describes which body parts a pathway controls (organs, glands, heart). It doesn't just mean "happens without thinking." Breathing uses skeletal muscle, so its motor neurons are somatic, even when it runs on autopilot.

2

The vagus nerve: the wanderer

There are 12 pairs of cranial nerves. They leave the brain directly instead of the spinal cord. Most of them stay in the head and neck. One of them doesn't: the vagus ("wandering") nerve travels all the way down into the chest and belly.

The mix-up

The vagus is the only cranial nerve that carries both sensory and motor fibers.

The vagus is the only cranial nerve involved in autonomic control.

The vagus nerve comes out of the spinal cord.

Actually

Several cranial nerves are mixed (sensory + motor), like the trigeminal and facial nerves.

Three other cranial nerves also carry parasympathetic fibers, but only to the head. What makes the vagus special is how far it reaches: it carries parasympathetic commands to the heart, lungs and gut, and brings sensory information from those organs back up.

It leaves the brainstem (medulla), like the other cranial nerves below the midbrain.

Follow the wanderer

Send signals down the vagus to the organs, or let the organs report back up.

The vagus leaves the medulla and wanders down through the neck to the chest and abdomen.

The 12 cranial nerves at a glance

S = sensory, M = motor, B = both. A heart marks the ones that carry parasympathetic fibers.

Remember it: Vagus = vagabond, the wanderer. It's the cranial nerve that leaves the head, carrying "rest and digest" commands down to the organs and organ sensations back up to the brainstem.

3

Bipolar vs. pseudounipolar neurons

Neurons are named by how many processes leave the cell body. Count only where each branch leaves the cell body, not how many branches there are farther out.

The mix-up

A pseudounipolar neuron has two long branches, so it must be bipolar.

Neurons are classified by how many dendritic spines or synapses they have.

Actually

Bipolar: two processes leave the cell body, one on each side.

Pseudounipolar: one stalk leaves the cell body and then splits into a T. It's "pseudo" (fake) unipolar because the single stalk turns into two branches.

Count only where branches leave the cell body

Remember it: Bipolar = bicycle, with one wheel on each side of the cell body. Pseudounipolar = a T: one stalk, then a split. Your touch and pain neurons are pseudounipolar. Bipolar neurons are in the retina, nose and inner ear.

4

Gray matter, white matter, and what multiple sclerosis breaks

Slice a brain and you see two colors. Gray matter is where neurons' cell bodies, dendrites and synapses are, so it's where information is processed. White matter is bundles of myelinated axons, the cables that carry signals between regions. Myelin is mostly fat, which is why it looks white.

The mix-up

Gray matter is made mostly of myelinated axons.

White matter does the brain's processing; gray matter just connects regions.

Multiple sclerosis damages neurons' ability to integrate (add up) their inputs.

Actually

Gray = cell bodies (processing). White = myelinated axons (wiring).

In multiple sclerosis (MS), the immune system attacks the myelin in the brain and spinal cord. Without myelin, signals slow down or fail, so MS is a problem of conduction, not integration.

Where's the gray, where's the white?

In the brain, gray is on the outside (the cortex). In the spinal cord, it's flipped. The circles zoom in to show what each one is made of.

Race the signal: healthy axon vs. MS

Set how much myelin MS has destroyed, then send a signal down both axons.

Remember it: Gray matter = the computers (cell bodies). White matter = the cables (myelinated axons). MS strips the insulation off the cables, so messages arrive late or not at all.

5

Slicing the brain: planes and the longitudinal fissure

There are three ways to slice a brain. A sagittal cut separates left from right. A coronal cut separates front from back. A horizontal cut separates top from bottom. Only one special cut, the midsagittal, gives two matching halves.

The mix-up

A horizontal plane always divides the brain into two equal, matching halves.

The longitudinal fissure separates the frontal lobe from the temporal (or parietal) lobe.

The thalamus sits right under the longitudinal fissure and connects the two hemispheres.

Actually

A horizontal cut can be made at any height, so the pieces usually aren't equal, and top and bottom never match. Only the midsagittal plane, right down the middle, makes mirror-image halves.

The longitudinal fissure is the deep groove between the left and right hemispheres. At its floor lies the corpus callosum, the band of axons that connects the two sides.

Slice it yourself

Pick a plane and drag the slider to move the cut. The two pieces are shown in different colors.

Remember it: Sagittal splits left from right (like an arrow, sagitta, shot between the eyes). Coronal is where a crown sits, front vs. back. Horizontal is the horizon, top vs. bottom. The longitudinal fissure runs the long way, front to back, between the hemispheres.

Finding structures on the midline view

The midsagittal view is the brain sliced down the middle, so you're looking at the inside face of one half. The front of the brain is on the left in this drawing. The most-missed labels were the lateral ventricle, corpus callosum, thalamus and hypothalamus.

The mix-up

Mixing up the corpus callosum with the lateral ventricle just under it.

Mixing up the thalamus with the hypothalamus.

Actually

The corpus callosum is the C-shaped band of white matter (axons) arching over the top. The lateral ventricle is the fluid-filled space tucked right under that arch.

The thalamus is the big oval in the center. Hypothalamus means "below the thalamus." It's the small region just underneath, sitting above the pituitary.

Remember it: The corpus callosum is the bridge on top. The ventricle is the pond under the bridge. The thalamus is the island in the middle. Hypo = below.

Basal ganglia, cerebellum, or limbic system?

Two of these three systems help with movement, so it's easy to blame the wrong one. Ask what kind of problem you're seeing: trouble starting or stopping movement, clumsy movement, or problems with feelings and memory.

The mix-up

Parkinson's disease is a cerebellum problem because it affects movement.

Actually

Basal ganglia: starting, stopping and choosing movements (Parkinson's, Huntington's).

Cerebellum: smoothness, timing and balance (clumsy, shaky reaching, unsteady walking).

Limbic system: emotion and memory (hippocampus, amygdala).

Sort the symptoms

Remember it: Basal ganglia = the start/stop button. Cerebellum = the autocorrect that smooths movement. Limbic = feelings and memories.

8

Broca's and Wernicke's areas

Language uses a whole network of areas, but two of them stand out. Broca's area in the frontal lobe is key for producing speech. Wernicke's area in the temporal lobe is key for understanding it.

The mix-up

Speaking should light up Wernicke's area most, because you have to understand words before you can say them.

Both areas light up equally no matter what you're doing.

Actually

Compare speaking minus listening and the area that stands out is Broca's. Compare listening minus speaking and Wernicke's (plus auditory cortex) stands out.

Turn on a task

Remember it: Broca = Building speech (front, near the mouth's motor area). Wernicke = What does it mean? (back, near hearing). Damage to Broca's area causes slow, effortful speech. Damage to Wernicke's area causes fluent speech that doesn't make sense.

9

Choosing a brain method, and Nissl vs. Golgi stains

Every method trades off two things: where (spatial resolution) and when (temporal resolution). The first question to ask is whether you need a picture of the brain's structure or a measure of its activity.

The mix-up

To check whether the hippocampus has shrunk, use fMRI or EEG. Those are "brain scans" too.

A Golgi stain shows how densely packed cells are, and a Nissl stain shows a neuron's branches.

Actually

Shrinkage is a question about structure, so use MRI. fMRI, PET and EEG measure activity.

Nissl stains the cell body of every cell, so you see density and layers. Golgi stains only a few neurons, but each one shows up completely, with all its branches.

Remember it: Nissl = Numbers (count cell bodies). Golgi = the Gorgeous whole neuron. For methods: "Structure? MRI. Exactly where it's active? fMRI. Exactly when? EEG. Is it needed? TMS."

10

Somatic or behavioral? Interventions and outcomes

Biological psychology experiments change one thing (the intervention) and measure another (the outcome). Each can be somatic, meaning about the body, or behavioral, meaning about what the person or animal does or experiences.

The mix-up

Measuring blood pressure counts as a behavioral outcome, because the person "did" something.

Any study that uses a drug is a behavioral study.

Actually

Somatic intervention: change the body directly, like a drug, a lesion, brain stimulation or a hormone.

Behavioral intervention: change experience, like training, a stressful situation or an enriched cage.

Behavioral outcome: what the organism does, like a memory score or time spent exploring. Heart rate, blood pressure and brain activity are somatic measures.

Sort them

Click an item, then click the box it belongs in. Wrong answers bounce back.

Remember it: Somatic = soma = body. If you could measure it with a needle, a cuff or a scanner, it's somatic. If you'd measure it with a stopwatch, a score or by watching what they do, it's behavioral. Example: give caffeine (somatic intervention) → memory test score (behavioral outcome).

The spinal cord, top to bottom

The five regions follow your body from the neck down: cervical → thoracic → lumbar → sacral → coccygeal.

The mix-up

Lumbar comes before thoracic, or the order starts at the tailbone.

Actually

Neck (Cervical), chest (Thoracic), lower back (Lumbar), pelvis (Sacral), tailbone (Coccygeal). There are 31 pairs of spinal nerves: 8 + 12 + 5 + 5 + 1.

12

Potassium and the resting potential

At rest, K⁺ is much more concentrated inside the neuron. K⁺ leaks out down that gradient and takes positive charge with it, which leaves the inside around −70 mV. If you change the K⁺ outside the cell, you change how hard it pushes out.

The mix-up

If extra K⁺ builds up outside (hyperkalemia), more K⁺ leaks out and the cell hyperpolarizes.

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump instantly fixes it, so nothing changes.

At rest the inside is positive because there's so much K⁺ in there.

Actually

More K⁺ outside makes the gradient smaller, so less K⁺ leaks out. Less positive charge leaves, so the cell depolarizes (becomes less negative).

The pump is slow and only rebuilds the gradients over time. It can't hold the resting potential in place.

The inside is negative at rest, precisely because positive K⁺ has leaked out.

Raise the potassium outside the cell

4 mM

Calculated with the Goldman equation using typical textbook concentrations (K⁺ inside 140 mM, Na⁺ outside 145 mM, inside 15 mM, resting Na⁺:K⁺ permeability 0.04:1). Normal K⁺ outside is about 4 mM.

Remember it: K⁺ leaking out is what makes the inside negative. Anything that shrinks that leak (like more K⁺ outside) makes the inside less negative.

13

Refractory periods and one-way travel

An action potential has two refractory periods. Right after a spike, Na⁺ channels are inactivated and can't reopen at any stimulus strength (the absolute refractory period). After that, the membrane is still a bit hyperpolarized, so a bigger-than-normal stimulus is needed (the relative refractory period). This is also why the signal only moves forward.

The mix-up

The axon hillock blocks signals from coming back, or Na⁺ ions can only flow toward the terminal.

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump causes repolarization.

In saltatory conduction, ions jump from node to node.

Actually

The membrane just behind the spike is still refractory, so the spike can only spread forward into fresh membrane.

Voltage-gated K⁺ channels opening, plus Na⁺ channels inactivating, cause repolarization. The pump is far too slow.

In a myelinated axon, electrical current spreads quickly under the myelin, and the spike is regenerated at each node. No ions jump.

What's happening at each moment

Why it only goes forward

Fire from the axon hillock as usual. Then zap the middle of the axon and watch what happens. The membrane itself can carry a spike in either direction.

■ firing   ■ absolute refractory   ■ relative refractory

Saltatory conduction: current spreads, the spike is rebuilt

Remember it: Na⁺ opens the door (rising phase). K⁺ closes the deal (falling phase). Behind every spike, the Na⁺ channels are locked for a moment. That's what keeps the signal moving forward.

The order of synaptic release (and gas transmitters)

Calcium is the trigger. Without Ca²⁺ coming into the terminal, vesicles don't fuse and no transmitter is released.

The mix-up

The action potential arrives, vesicles fuse, and then Ca²⁺ channels open.

Gas transmitters like nitric oxide work like the others, just smaller.

Actually

Spike arrives → Ca²⁺ channels open → vesicles fuse → transmitter binds receptors → transmitter is cleared.

Gas transmitters are made on demand and are never stored in vesicles. They diffuse straight through cell membranes.

15

Ionotropic vs. metabotropic receptors

Ionotropic receptors are ion channels: transmitter binds, the channel opens, and the effect is fast and brief. Metabotropic receptors aren't channels. They start a G-protein chain reaction inside the cell, so the effect is slower and longer-lasting.

The mix-up

The nicotinic receptor at the muscle is metabotropic.

GABA-A makes an IPSP by letting Na⁺ in.

Actually

The nicotinic ACh receptor is ionotropic (a ligand-gated channel). The muscarinic receptor is metabotropic.

GABA-A is a channel that lets Cl⁻ in. Negative charge enters, the inside gets more negative, and that's an IPSP.

Race them

Remember it: Ionotropic = the receptor is the ion door. Metabotropic = a middleman (the G protein). Nicotinic = Now (fast). Muscarinic = Middleman.

Acetylcholine: one transmitter, opposite effects

The same ACh molecule makes skeletal muscle contract but makes the heart slow down. The difference comes from the receptor, not the transmitter.

The mix-up

ACh turns into an inhibitory chemical before it reaches the heart.

Loewi's experiment showed that nerves signal electrically.

Donepezil works by blocking ACh reuptake.

Donepezil cures Alzheimer's by replacing lost neurons.

Actually

Muscle has nicotinic receptors (Na⁺ in, contract). The heart has muscarinic receptors (G protein, K⁺ out, slower beat).

Loewi showed that a chemical in the fluid (ACh, which he called "vagus substance") slowed a second heart. That's how we know synapses are chemical.

ACh isn't taken back up. It's broken down in the synapse by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, and donepezil blocks that enzyme.

Donepezil only helps the surviving cholinergic neurons get more out of their ACh. It eases symptoms; it doesn't stop the disease.

Same key, different locks

Loewi's two frog hearts (1921)

Otto Loewi kept two frog hearts beating in dishes of salty fluid. Only heart 1 still had its vagus nerve attached.

Both hearts beat at their normal rate.

What is donepezil?

What it is
A prescription pill for Alzheimer's disease, sold as Aricept and taken once a day. It's one of the most commonly prescribed Alzheimer's drugs.
Drug type
An acetylcholinesterase inhibitor: it blocks the enzyme that breaks down ACh. It crosses the blood–brain barrier, so it works in the brain.
What it does
Leaves more ACh in the synapses for longer. That can modestly help memory and thinking.
What it doesn't do
It doesn't cure Alzheimer's, stop neurons from dying, or clear plaques and tangles. It treats symptoms while the disease keeps going.
Side effects
Nausea, diarrhea, vivid dreams, and a slower heartbeat. Extra ACh reaches the heart's muscarinic receptors too, just like Loewi's "vagus substance."

Why give it? The "too little ACh" idea of Alzheimer's

Pick a brain and watch the ACh (gold dots) travel from the basal forebrain to the cortex.

Zoom in on one synapse: donepezil protects the ACh that's left

Remember it: The transmitter is the key, the receptor is the lock, and the lock decides what happens. ACh is cleared by an enzyme, not by reuptake. Alzheimer's = fewer ACh neurons; donepezil = make the ACh you still have last longer.

17

Transmitter families and which drug hits which

Monoamines are the big family. Catecholamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine) are a branch of it made from tyrosine. Serotonin is also a monoamine, but it's made from tryptophan, so it's not a catecholamine.

The mix-up

Serotonin is a catecholamine.

LSD and psilocybin are dopamine drugs.

Actually

All catecholamines are monoamines, but not every monoamine is a catecholamine. Serotonin is the odd one out.

Classic psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline) mainly activate serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.

The family tree, and the assembly line

Both start as amino acids from the protein in your food. Tryptophan is an essential amino acid: your body can't make it, so it has to come from your diet. Tyrosine comes from food too, and your liver can also make it from another amino acid, phenylalanine. Fun fact: tyrosine is named after the Greek word for cheese (tyros), because it was first found in cheese. Myth check: turkey doesn't make you sleepy because of tryptophan. It has about the same amount as chicken. The big meal is the real cause.

Drug → target

Remember it: Tyrosine → DOPA → Dopamine → Norepinephrine → Epinephrine. "Tiny Dogs Don't Need Exercise." Psychedelics → serotonin.

18

Turning a synapse up or down

After a transmitter is released, three things decide how much signal gets through: how long it stays in the cleft, how fast enzymes destroy it, and what's sitting on the receptors. Most drugs work by changing one of these.

The mix-up

Blocking the reuptake transporter makes enzymes break down more transmitter, so signaling drops.

Speeding up the enzymes that break down a transmitter increases signaling.

An antagonist is a weak agonist: it binds and partly turns the receptor on.

Actually

Block reuptake → transmitter stays in the cleft longer → more signaling (SSRIs, cocaine).

More enzyme activity → transmitter destroyed faster → less signaling. Blocking the enzyme (MAO inhibitors, donepezil) gives more.

An antagonist binds the receptor (it has affinity) but doesn't activate it (zero efficacy), so it blocks the transmitter. A partial agonist is the one that turns the receptor on partway.

Remember it: Anything that makes transmitter stay longer (block reuptake, block the enzyme) turns the signal up. Anything that destroys it faster turns it down. Antagonist = a key that fits the lock but won't turn.

19

Dopamine pathways and antipsychotics

Dopamine neurons in the midbrain send fibers along separate highways, and each highway does a different job. A drug that blocks D2 receptors everywhere hits all of them at once.

The mix-up

Haloperidol's tremor and stiffness come from blocking the mesolimbic (reward) pathway.

Parkinson's disease is caused by losing dopamine in the reward pathway.

Actually

Blocking D2 in the mesolimbic pathway is the goal (fewer hallucinations and delusions). Blocking it in the mesostriatal (nigrostriatal) pathway causes the movement side effects.

Parkinson's happens when the substantia nigra neurons of the mesostriatal (movement) pathway die.

Three highways from the midbrain

Remember it: Striatal = stride (movement). Limbic = liking and wanting (reward). Cortical = cognition (planning, focus). Haloperidol blocks all three, so the helpful effect comes with side effects.

20

Reading a dose-response curve

Two numbers describe most dose-response curves. Potency is how much drug you need, shown by the curve's left-right position (its ED50). Efficacy is how big the effect can get, shown by the curve's maximum height.

The mix-up

A drug with a bigger ED50 is more potent.

Tolerance lowers the curve's maximum, or shifts the curve left.

Same ED50 means the same safety.

Actually

Lower ED50 = more potent, because a smaller dose gets the job done. That puts the curve farther left.

Tolerance shifts the curve right (you need more drug), and the maximum usually stays the same. Sensitization shifts it left.

Safety depends on the therapeutic index (LD50 ÷ ED50), the gap between a helpful dose and a deadly one.

Change the drug, watch the curve

Therapeutic index: same ED50, very different safety

Remember it: Left = less needed = more potent. Height = efficacy. Tolerance moves the curve right, sensitization moves it left. A narrow therapeutic index means overdose is easy.

Tolerance traps and alcohol's two phases

Tolerance doesn't build evenly for every effect of a drug. With alcohol, early "excitement" comes from switching off the brain's brakes, not from stimulating the brain.

The mix-up

Opioid users become tolerant to breathing suppression, so they raise the dose for the high.

Alcohol is a stimulant at first and becomes a depressant later.

Actually

It's the reverse. Tolerance to the high and pain relief builds quickly, but tolerance to breathing suppression builds slowly. Raising the dose for the same high moves the user toward the dose that stops breathing.

Alcohol is a depressant the whole time (it boosts GABA-A and blocks NMDA). Low doses quiet the prefrontal brakes first, so behavior looks excited. Higher doses slow everything.

The opioid tolerance gap

0

The numbers are made up to show the idea; real doses vary widely from person to person.

Alcohol: releasing the brakes, then slowing everything down

0.04

Why alcohol plus benzodiazepines is dangerous

Remember it: Chasing the same high is the trap, because breathing suppression doesn't build tolerance at the same pace. A new setting is another risk: without the usual cues, the body's learned (conditioned) tolerance doesn't kick in. Alcohol releases the brakes first, then slows everything down. Two depressants together add up.
